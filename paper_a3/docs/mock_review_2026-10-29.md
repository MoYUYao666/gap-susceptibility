# Mock review: "What does the text buy? Controlled text-provenance experiments for remote-sensing vision–language models"

Reviewer profile: one remote-sensing VLM researcher, one evaluation/benchmarking researcher.
Manuscript version: 23 pages, 10 tables, 2 figures, 30 references (the version with the cloud supplements integrated).
Verdict I would give: **major revision** (at a top venue: borderline reject; at ISPRS J: plausible major revision).

The five comments below are ordered by how much damage they do, not by how easy they are to fix.

## R1 (major). The paper measures, it does not propose anything

The manuscript holds a frozen ViT-B/32 plus LoRA fixed and varies only the text source. This is careful work, but a
controlled comparison of four text sources, on one retrieval protocol, with an off-the-shelf backbone and an
off-the-shelf adapter, is a study, not a contribution the field cannot already guess. The paper is 23 pages for what
is, in essence, an ablation table plus robustness checks. What is the new capability a reader gains beyond "results
depend on the data"?

*Current defense:* the conditioning itself is the result (rankings flip with schedule, budget and target domain), the
study is pre-registered in hypotheses H1–H4, and every number is reproducible in 15 GPU-hours. The protocol is a
concrete deliverable.

*Minimal fix:* sharpen the opening so that the contribution is stated as a *claim about the literature* ("the four
sources have never been compared under matched conditions; three of the four published claims about them do not
survive matching") rather than as "we run a careful comparison". Add one sentence naming what a practitioner should do
differently on Monday.

## R2 (major). No language-model-generated caption arm — the source the field actually uses

The four arms are human captions, parsed objects, templates and metadata. In 2026 the dominant source is captions
generated by a large language model (GAIA uses 201k GPT-4o captions; other 2026 work uses LLM-curated captions). The
paper cannot say anything about the most relevant arm. The human-rich arm brackets length/density, not phrasing.

*Current defense:* none in the manuscript; it is a genuine scope hole.

*Minimal fix:* state the gap explicitly in the Limitations (done in the revision) and add one sentence to the
introduction explaining why the LLM arm was not included (a frozen caption set would have to be generated and verified,
which changes the paper into a data-generation study). If the authors can obtain public LLM captions for one corpus,
one extra arm would substantially raise the paper's value.

## R3 (major). The RSITMD comparison is not budget-matched

Table 2 reports exactly 10,000 pairs per cell, but the derived arms produce one text per image. On RSITMD the human
corpus has 22,015 pairs while each derived corpus has 4,743 rows; the run records show the human arm training on 7,252
pairs against 3,435 for every derived arm. The paper's central claim for RSITMD ("human text loses") therefore comes
from a comparison where the human arm has twice the pairs. Section 3 says the ceiling is reported "rather than hidden",
but it is not reported in the results.

*Current defense:* the reversal survives with twice the data, which actually strengthens "human text is not the
right granularity for RSITMD"; but the paper does not make that argument.

*Minimal fix (applied):* one sentence in the results with the two effective pair counts, framed as a check that the
reversal is not a starvation artefact.

## R4 (moderate). Statistical claims rest on three seeds and no test

The paper reports mean ± sd over three seeds and then ranks arms. For the RSITMD segmented-versus-templated ordering
the gap (0.167 vs 0.175, both towers) is the same size as the largest seed sd (0.018), yet the text says templated is
"best". No confidence interval, no paired test, 144 cells compared.

*Current defense:* the dispersion is reported for every cell, which is more than most papers in this area do.

*Minimal fix (applied):* explicitly refuse to interpret the one ordering that is inside dispersion, and state that every
ordering the argument relies on separates by at least three standard deviations.

## R5 (moderate). Only one task, and the second task is a toy probe

The paper measures retrieval on four corpora and a captioning probe with a deliberately small decoder. Practitioners
care about detection, segmentation, VQA and instruction following; the paper's claim is that provenance matters for
"remote-sensing vision–language models", but the evidence covers one task family.

*Current defense:* the Limitations now say the task range is retrieval plus a probe; the captioning probe is presented
as a direction test, not a level.

*Minimal fix:* narrow the title/abstract claim from "vision–language models" to "retrieval-oriented vision–language
models" if the task range is not extended; or add one downstream fine-tuning experiment (the same adapters, a frozen
backbone, a small detector or a VQA head) which is cheap in this setup.

## Minor comments that would also appear

- The four derived arms for three corpora are built from those corpora's own human captions; only DIOR-RSVG has an
  independent source. The paper says this, but a reader may still read the comparison as four independent sources.
- Absolute R@1 values are low (0.03–0.21) and no published baseline is reproduced; the paper correctly avoids calling
  them field numbers, but a reader may ask whether the ordering holds at higher absolute performance.
- One sentence in the earlier version mixed up the DIOR best arm and its number; it has been corrected (parsed-object
  0.136, not 0.033).
- Reference list now contains 30 entries including two 2026 works (LLM-curated farm captions; MLLM caption fusion);
  both are positioned as "text as a resource, not a variable".

## What this mock review means for submission

Two of the five major comments cannot be fixed by editing (R1 in part, R2 in full); they define the paper's ceiling at
a general venue and are the most likely cause of a desk rejection at ISPRS J. Three are now pre-empted in the text
(R3, R4) or in the Limitations (R5, part of R1). The realistic outcomes at ISPRS J are: desk reject on novelty (~35%),
major revision with a request for an LLM arm or a downstream task (~40%), minor-to-major revision on presentation
(~25%).

## Length control (added after this review)

A control arm that repeats the same human caption five times, matched in length to the human-rich arm, was run
(6 cells, 3 seeds): RSICD 0.127 +- 0.007 and NWPU-Captions 0.217 +- 0.007, against human 0.148/0.165 and human-rich
0.196/0.340. The density gain is therefore not a length effect; this closes the "human-rich just has more tokens"
objection and is now stated in Section 5.3.

## LLM arm added after this review (closes R2, partly)

3,000 RSICD images were captioned once with a local Qwen3-VL-8B (1.44 s/img) and all four arms were retrained on the
same 3,000 images: human 0.117 +- 0.004, LLM 0.104 +- 0.004, templated 0.037 +- 0.003, metadata 0.008 +- 0.001.
R2 is now a scope note (one corpus, one generator, 3,000 images) rather than a missing arm; the claim that LLM captions
behave like dense human descriptions is supported.

## Second corpus and second task (added 2026-10-30)

The LLM arm was extended to NWPU-Captions (968 images on the five-arm intersection): LLM 0.233 +- 0.001 against human
0.151 +- 0.000, templated 0.077 and metadata 0.017, i.e. the LLM arm overtakes human where the human captions are short.
A classification probe on the adapted encoders saturates (0.965/0.964/0.969), which is reported as a boundary
condition: the provenance effect is a retrieval-level effect at this scale. R2 is now limited to "two subsets, one
generator"; R5 is answered with an explicit null result rather than silence.

## Full-scale LLM arms (added 2026-10-30, closes R2)

Both generators were run on all 10,921 RSICD images: Qwen3-VL-8B 0.262 +- 0.010, MiniCPM-V-2.6 0.159 +- 0.004,
against human 0.127 +- 0.002 and human-rich 0.202 +- 0.002. R2 is now a scope note only (NWPU LLM evidence remains on a
968-image subset; two generators, not a generator ranking).
