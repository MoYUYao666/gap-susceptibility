ABSTRACT
Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Digital Twin (DT) technologies are transforming maintenance
strategies across engineering disciplines, yet their integrated application to marine machinery
systems remains fragmented and unsynthesized. This paper addresses this gap through a
comprehensive systematic review of AI and DT enabled intelligent maintenance for marine
machinery. The AI contribution is a systematic taxonomy spanning five evolutionary gen-
erations—Traditional Machine Learning, Deep Learning, Transformer-based Architectures,
FoundationModels,andPhysics-InformedAI—appliedtofaultdiagnosis,remainingusefullife
prediction, and maintenance decision-making. The engineering application is a unified five-
layer Marine Intelligent Maintenance Digital Twin Framework that integrates physical ship
systems, sensor data acquisition, digital twin modeling, AI-based prediction, and maintenance
decision-making,withexplicitaccommodationofMaritimeAutonomousSurfaceShips(MASS)
autonomyrequirements.Synthesizing133publicationsfrom2015tomid-2026retrievedthrough
a systematic search guided by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and
Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 framework, the review further identifies seven critical chal-
lenges—data scarcity, AI interpretability, cross-vessel generalization, real-time edge deploy-
ment, uncertainty quantification, autonomous maintenance for MASS, and cybersecurity—and
proposes a four-phase development roadmap from AI-assisted diagnosis to fully autonomous
maintenance agents.
1. Introduction
1.1. Background and Motivation
The global maritime industry carries approximately 80% of world trade volume across more than 100,000
commercialvessels,formingthebackboneofinternationalcommerce[1,2,3].Thereliabilityofshipmachinery—the
main propulsion engine, auxiliary power systems, and associated rotating equipment—directly determines the safety,
economic efficiency, and environmental sustainability of maritime operations [4, 5].
Shipmachineryoperatesunderexceptionallydemandingconditions.Prolongedcontinuousoperation,harshmarine
environmentswithhighhumidityandsaltspray,temperatureextremes,wideandvariableoperatingenvelopesspanning
harbor maneuvering to full-speed ocean transit, and severely limited accessibility for at-sea inspection and repair
combine to accelerate degradation and precipitate unexpected failures [6]. Unplanned machinery downtime at sea
incurseconomiccostsestimatedattenstohundredsofthousandsofdollarsperdayforlargecommercialvesselswhile
simultaneously compromising crew safety, cargo integrity, and environmental protection [7].
Traditional maintenance strategies for ship machinery follow two paradigms. Corrective maintenance—repair
after failure—results in unplanned downtime, secondary damage from cascading failures, and the highest lifecycle
costs. Time-based preventive maintenance—scheduled overhauls at fixed intervals—avoids unscheduled downtime
but often dismantles healthy equipment unnecessarily while potentially missing degradation that accelerates between
inspections[6,8,9,10].Bothapproachesarefundamentallyreactive.Neitherleveragesthewealthofoperationaldata
generated by modern sensor-equipped vessels.
Intelligentmaintenance—integratingconditionmonitoring,faultdiagnosis,remainingusefullife(RUL)prediction,
andmaintenancedecisionoptimization—representsaparadigmshiftfrom“fail-and-fix”to“predict-and-prevent”[11,
12]. Two technological pillars that have matured substantially in the past decade enable this transition: artificial
intelligence (AI) and digital twin (DT) technology.

1.3. The Engineering Problem: Marine Machinery Maintenance
Despite these technological advances, the practical deployment of AI- and DT-enabled intelligent maintenance in
the maritime industry remains nascent—the gap between academic demonstration and industrial deployment is wider
than in aerospace or manufacturing [29, 30]. Three converging factors drive the need for systematic synthesis.
First, the autonomous ship imperative. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) has defined four Degrees
of Autonomy (DoA) for Maritime Autonomous Surface Ships (MASS), ranging from automated decision support
(DoA 1) to fully autonomous operation without onboard crew (DoA 4) [31]. At DoA 3 and DoA 4, vessels must
operatereliablywithoutcrewforextendedperiods—theMUNINprojectanticipatedunattendedengineroomoperation
as a design target rather than a demonstrated capability [32]. Reliability analysis of a modelled vessel configuration
indicates that safe operation without human intervention decreases from approximately 47 weeks at DoA 2 to only 3
weeksatDoA 4undercurrentmaintenance paradigms;theseare scenario-modeloutputsfroma singlestudy,notfleet
measurements [33].
Second, marine machinery complexity. Unlike laboratory rotating-machinery testbeds, ship machinery encom-
passespropulsionsystems,powersystems,andauxiliarysystems,eachwithdistinctfailuremodes,degradationmech-

bearings and gears—rather than system-level integration.
Third, unique data challenges. Fault data is exceptionally scarce because critical components are preventively
replaced before failure and operators treat operational data as proprietary [28]. Ships operate under highly variable

Satellite communication at sea is expensive, bandwidth-limited, and weather-vulnerable, constraining cloud-based

1.6. Review Methodology
A systematic literature search following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-
Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [38] was conducted in July 2026 across ScienceDirect, IEEE Xplore, Springer,
MDPI, Semantic Scholar, and Chinese academic databases (CNKI, Chinese Journal of Ship Research). Scopus and
Web of Science were not included in the formal search owing to institutional access constraints; this limitation
is acknowledged in Section 8. Search terms spanned four concept groups—domain, technology, application, and
cross-cutting methods—combined with Boolean operators; the full search strings for each database are provided as
supplementary material. From 250 initial publications, 215 unique records remained after deduplication, 190 passed
title and abstract screening, and 133 were included in the final review after full-text eligibility assessment. Inclusion
criteria required studies applying AI or DT techniques to fault diagnosis, RUL prediction, or maintenance decision-
making for marine machinery, published between 2015 and mid-2026, written in English or Chinese, and published
as peer-reviewed journal articles, conference papers, or authoritative technical reports; studies addressing land-based

2. Marine Machinery Systems and Maintenance Challenges
2.1. Propulsion Systems
Marine diesel engines divide into two categories: two-stroke low-speed engines (crosshead type, directly coupled
topropeller,60–250RPM)andfour-strokemedium-speedengines(trunkpistontype,drivingthroughagearbox,400–
1000 RPM) [40]. Common failure modes encompass combustion chamber faults (piston ring wear, cylinder liner
scuffing, exhaust valve leakage, injector clogging), turbocharger faults (compressor fouling, turbine blade erosion,
bearing degradation), fuel system faults (injector nozzle erosion, common rail pressure anomalies), cooling system
faults (intercooler efficiency loss, heat exchanger fouling), and lubrication system faults (oil pump wear, filter
clogging) [17, 41]. Kim et al. [42] constructed a reliability-based fault database comprising 109 fault simulation sets
for marine engine auxiliary equipment, establishing condition-based maintenance (CBM) and PHM as key enablers
for MASS development.
Shaft systems transmit power from engine to propeller through intermediate shafts, stern tube bearings, thrust
bearings, and couplings. Principal failure modes include bearing wear (white metal degradation from oil film
breakdown), shaft misalignment (parallel and angular), fatigue cracks at stress concentration points, and coupling

3. Evolution of Marine Maintenance Strategies
The evolution of marine maintenance follows a clear trajectory from reactive to proactive paradigms. Table 4
provides a structured comparison.
3.1. From Corrective to Intelligent Maintenance
Corrective Maintenance (CM).Equipment is operated until failure, then repaired or replaced. Though simple to
administer, CM produces unplanned downtime, secondary damage from cascading failures, and the highest lifecycle
costs. At sea, where repair resources and spare parts are severely limited, CM is especially hazardous [6, 8].
Time-Based Preventive Maintenance (TBM).Interventions are scheduled at fixed intervals—running hours
or calendar time—irrespective of actual equipment condition. The Planned Maintenance System mandated by
classification societies is the dominant implementation. TBM avoids unscheduled downtime but may unnecessarily
dismantle healthy equipment while missing degradation that accelerates between inspection intervals [9].
Condition-BasedMaintenance(CBM).Real-timeconditionmonitoringtriggersmaintenanceinterventionswhen
indicators of impending failure are detected. The Vehicle Health Management System concept, originating from
DNV’s reliability-centred maintenance framework [57], integrates real-time monitoring of propulsion, auxiliary,
boiler, shafting, steering, and ballast systems through sensor networks supplemented by CCTV and thermal cameras.
Condition-based monitoring of operating medium-speed diesel engines was demonstrated on real vessels as early as
2015 [58], and CBM remains the minimum viable maintenance strategy for autonomous ships [32].
PredictiveMaintenance(PdM).PdMextendsCBMbyincorporatingprognosticmodelsthatforecastdegradation
trajectories and RUL, enabling maintenance scheduling with optimal lead time. In the maritime context, PdM
is reported to reduce maintenance operating expenditure substantially compared with conventional schemes [35].
Enabling technologies include physics-based degradation models, data-driven machine learning and deep learning
prognostics,andhybridphysics-datafusionapproaches[17,18].TheMUNINprojectpioneeredtheunmannedengine
room concept as a design target rather than a demonstrated capability [59].
Intelligent Maintenance (IM).IM integrates CBM, PdM, and automated decision support within an AI-driven
framework that autonomously detects anomalies, diagnoses fault types, predicts RUL, and recommends optimal
maintenance actions with quantified uncertainty [12, 20]. For MASS at DoA 3 and DoA 4, where no crew is present,
IM with automated decision execution transitions toward autonomous maintenance.

literature, we map autonomy degrees to maintenance requirements as follows. At DoA 1 (decision support with

4. AI Techniques for Marine Machinery Maintenance
This section constitutes the technical core of the review, presenting a systematic taxonomy of AI techniques
applied to marine machinery maintenance across five paradigm waves. The waves are classified by dominant learning
paradigm and marine adoption window rather than by strict succession, because the underlying methods coexist and
interpenetrateintime.Wetracetheprogressionfromtraditionalmachinelearningthroughdeeplearning,Transformer
architectures, and foundation models to Physics-Informed AI, analysing each wave’s representative algorithms,
application domains, performance characteristics, and limitations. Table 5 provides a structured comparison across
all five generations.
4.1. Wave 1: Traditional Machine Learning (2015–2019)
Traditionalmachinelearningmethodsformedthefoundationofintelligentfaultdiagnosisbeforethedeeplearning
revolution. Self-organising map clustering of marine engine condition monitoring data was demonstrated as early as
2018 [63], establishing the viability of unsupervised learning on shipboard archives. These methods rely on explicit
feature engineering: domain experts extract statistical features (mean, variance, kurtosis, skewness) and frequency-
domain features (FFT magnitudes, envelope spectrum peaks) from raw sensor signals before feeding them into
classification or regression models.
4.1.1. Support Vector Machine (SVM)
SVM emerged as the most effective traditional method for marine diesel engine fault diagnosis. Vorkapić et
al. [64] compared linear regression, multilayer perceptron, SVM, and Random Forest for performance monitoring

diagnosis, demonstrating the method’s continued relevance alongside newer deep learning approaches.
4.1.4. Limitations and Transition
Traditional ML methods face inherent limitations: manual feature engineering is time-consuming and domain-
expertise-intensive, and may miss subtle fault signatures not captured by predefined statistical features. These
limitationsmotivatedthetransitiontodeeplearningmethodscapableofautomatichierarchicalfeatureextractionfrom
raw sensor data.
4.2. Wave 2: Deep Learning (2018–2022)
Deep learning revolutionized marine machinery fault diagnosis by enabling end-to-end learning: models learn
fault-relevant features directly from raw or minimally preprocessed sensor signals, eliminating the manual feature
engineering bottleneck.
4.2.1. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)
CNNs have become the dominant architecture for vibration-based fault diagnosis of marine rotating machinery.
Thecoreprincipleconvertsone-dimensionalvibrationtime-seriessignalsintotwo-dimensionalrepresentations(time-
frequency spectrograms via Short-Time Fourier Transform, wavelet transforms, or Gram angular fields), which
convolutional layers then process to learn hierarchical spatial feature representations.
Hybrid architectures combining convolutional and temporal-convolutional layers with attention mechanisms have
been widely adopted for marine engine vibration diagnosis, capturing local fault features while modelling temporal
dependencies across operating conditions. Hu et al. [68] applied a one-dimensional CNN to vibration data from
measurement points optimized through correlation analysis with information entropy, achieving 96.67% accuracy for
early combustion chamber fault classification and 97.35% for health status recognition.

Validated on real marine diesel engine datasets across three operating conditions, this hybrid approach outperformed
existing methods.

4.3. Wave 3: Transformer Architectures (2022–2024)

learning and cross-attention for multi-sensor rotating machinery diagnosis within a personalized federated learning
framework.

4.5. Wave 5: Physics-Informed AI (2019–Present, Marine Surge 2025–)
Physics-informed methods are Janus-faced: physics-based degradation modelling is the oldest prognostics class
in this domain (Section 3), while physics-informed neural networks as such date to 2019 and have surged in
marine applications since 2025. This wave embeds domain knowledge—thermodynamic laws, mechanical principles,
degradation physics—directly into neural network architectures and training objectives to address the fundamental
limitations of purely data-driven approaches: physical inconsistency, poor generalization under distribution shift, and
lack of interpretability.

data-driven partial differential equation into the Bayesian training objective, using a Weibull likelihood function for
time-to-failure modelling with complex-valued hierarchical GRUs.

TheSTRIDEframework[23]addressesthechallengingproblemofmulti-sourcedegradationdecouplinginmarine

5. Digital Twin Enabled Intelligent Maintenance
Digital twin technology represents the second technological pillar of intelligent marine machinery maintenance.
It complements AI by providing the high-fidelity virtual representations, synthetic data generation, and physics-based
constraints that AI models require. This section analyses DT architectures for marine applications, proposes a unified
Marine Intelligent Maintenance DT Framework, and examines the mechanisms of virtual-physical collaboration.
5.1. Digital Twin Fundamentals for Marine Machinery
Adigitaltwin,asdefinedbyGrievesandVickers[24]andextendedbyTaoetal.[25],comprisesfivedimensions:
the physical entity, the virtual model, the data connection between them, the service layer, and the DT data. In the

brationandstress,fluiddynamicsofcoolingandlubrication,andelectricalsignaturesofgeneratorsandmotors—across

Marine DTs face challenges distinct from manufacturing DTs. The physical asset is mobile and often beyond
reliable communication range. Operating conditions vary dramatically. Sensor installations must withstand salt spray,
vibration, humidity, and electromagnetic interference [37].

5.3. Proposed Framework: Marine Intelligent Maintenance DT Framework
Buildingonthearchitecturalanalysisabove,weproposeaunifiedfive-layerMarineIntelligentMaintenanceDigital

Layer 1: Physical Ship.The foundation layer comprises all monitored machinery: marine diesel engines, shaft
systems and bearings, gearboxes, generators and electrical systems, battery energy storage, pumps and compressors,
and HVAC. Each component is instrumented with appropriate sensors determined by its failure modes and criticality.
The marine specificity of the framework resides in three architectural design choices. First, the tiered data
acquisition strategy in Layer 2 explicitly accommodates maritime satellite communication constraints (bandwidth,
latency, cost), unlike generic Industry 4.0 architectures that assume always-on broadband connectivity. Second, the
Hybrid DT architecture in Layer 3 is motivated by the uniquely wide operating envelopes of ships (10–100% MCR,
tropicaltoarcticambient,ladentoballastdraft),underwhichpurelydata-drivenmodelsfailandpurelyphysics-based
models are too computationally expensive for real-time use. The adaptive weighting𝜆(𝑡)driven by real-time error
and uncertainty metrics provides robustness against these distribution shifts. Third, the Maintenance Decision layer
(Layer 5) explicitly gates its outputs by IMO DoA, with autonomous execution permitted only at DoA 4 and Remote
Control Centre-approved execution mandated at DoA 3 and below, consistent with IMO’s autonomy definitions. This
regulatory coupling is absent from generic PHM architectures.

6. Applications in Marine Machinery
This section organizes the literature by equipment type, covering marine diesel engines, bearing and shaft
systems, pumps and auxiliary equipment, and electrical power systems. Table 9 provides a consolidated summary
of representative cases.
6.1. Marine Diesel Engine
The marine diesel engine is the most intensively studied equipment category in marine machinery fault detection
and diagnosis research [34]. Applications span combustion monitoring, fault diagnosis, and long-term degradation
prediction.
6.1.1. Combustion Monitoring and Fault Diagnosis
Vibration-based diagnosis dominates the literature because engine vibration signals capture combustion events,
valve impacts, and piston slap with high information density. Recent hybrid architectures combining convolutional,
temporal-convolutional, and attention mechanisms have reported strong classification accuracy under realistic noise
conditions. Multi-layer attention fusion with CNN-LSTM and EWMA integration has demonstrated predictive
capability across three operating conditions [16].
Data imbalance is addressed through GAN-based augmentation, which synthesizes fault samples to balance the
severely skewed normal-to-fault ratio characteristic of shipboard archives [72]. Physics-based condition monitoring
using multivariate polynomial modelling with residual-based threshold alarming has effectively detected misfire
(exhaust temperature drops from 100–150◦C to 70–80◦C) and exhaust valve leakage (engine body vibration RMS
increasing nearly tenfold) on a marine diesel engine simulator [104].
6.1.2. Degradation Prediction and Health Prognosis
Long-termhealthprognosisofmarineengineshasbeenadvancedsignificantlybyDT-basedapproaches.Jeonand
Theotokatos [17] demonstrated that integrating thermodynamic models with stochastic degradation and data-driven
model management improves health indicator forecast R2 from approximately 0.25 to 0.94. Liu et al. [80] achieved
continuousdegradationmonitoringofinjector,fuelsystem,andvalvetrainwearusingonlycommonlyavailablesensors
throughNSGA-IIIinverse optimization.Mar-RUL [18]provides apractical ensembleframework combiningMarkov-
CNN, one-dimensional CNN, and LSTM architectures, validated on a tanker ship diesel generator turbocharger. For
ship operators, vibration-based CNN diagnosis of diesel engine faults is currently the most deployable AI capability,
with demonstrated robustness under realistic noise conditions, although shipboard validation on operating vessels
remains pending; the principal barrier to operational deployment is not algorithmic accuracy but the absence of
standardized fault databases spanning engine types and operating profiles.

regimes on a physical propulsion shafting test bench [43].

6.3. Pumps and Auxiliary Equipment
Pump condition monitoring has seen significant recent advances. Deep learning methods consistently outperform
traditionalML:aDCNNwithtwo-dimensionalimageconversionfromvibrationsignalsachieved99.07%classification

signal processing has been demonstrated as an alternative to manual leak inspection [110]. For ship operators, pump
cavitation detection using vibration-based deep learning is deployable today on vessels with existing vibration sensor
infrastructure; the LNG pump CNN-Transformer hybrid demonstrates that multi-fault classification at production-
grade accuracy is feasible with current technology.
6.4. Electrical Power Systems
Marine electrical system intelligent maintenance is a rapidly growing research area driven by shipping electrifi-
cation. Senemmar [48] developed a comprehensive deep-learning-based framework integrating Non-Intrusive Load
Monitoring (CNN, above 98% accuracy), fault detection (Wavelet Graph Neural Network, above 99% accuracy),
and autonomous reconfiguration (graph-based reinforcement learning) for medium-voltage direct current (MVDC)
shipboard power systems.
Battery energy storage system health management has emerged as critical for hybrid and all-electric vessels. A
hybrid deep learning framework (MATGAN+TDCN-Mamba) for joint state-of-health and RUL estimation achieved
58.4% reduction in SOH MAE and 61.9% reduction in RUL MAE while handling missing sensor data [111]. ICPO-
Bi-LSTM, validated on the all-electric ferry “Jun Lv Hao”, achieved R2 = 0.9999 for capacity prognostics under
dynamic conditions [52]. Battery management system fault diagnosis using DBSCAN clustering and iTransformer
models enables early detection of cell voltage anomalies [50].
A 2024 milestone was the regulatory acceptance of data-driven state-of-health testing as an alternative to annual
physical capacity tests, with ECM-based methods validated on multi-year operational data from six ships showing
maximum SOH deviation of only 3.21% [51]. For electric propulsion systems, gearbox fault diagnosis using Multi-
PathDynamicCNNunderstrongnoisyenvironmentshasbeenreportedformarineelectricthrustergearboxes.Forship
operators, battery SOH monitoring is the most mature electrical-system AI application, having achieved regulatory
acceptance for physical-test replacement in 2024; generator and motor fault diagnosis, while accurate in laboratory
settings, awaits shipboard validation programmes.
6.5. Cross-Equipment Observations
Several patterns emerge from the equipment-specific analysis. First, deep learning methods have achieved near-
ceiling accuracy (above 98%) on controlled experimental datasets across all equipment types, but performance under
realshipboardconditionswithnoise,transientoperation,anddistributionshiftremainssubstantiallylowerandunder-
validated. Second, the availability of labelled fault data varies dramatically by equipment type: diesel engines benefit
fromdecadesofdocumentedfailuremodes,whilstbatterysystemsandpowerelectronicshavelimitedmarine-specific
fault data. Third, physics-based approaches are most developed for diesel engines, where thermodynamic models are
mature,andleastdevelopedforelectricalsystems,whereelectromagnetic-thermal-mechanicalmulti-physicscoupling
complicates modelling. Fourth, edge deployment feasibility has been demonstrated for all equipment types, but
integrated multi-equipment edge platforms remain aspirational.

reported—a publication bias that likely overstates the practical readiness of marine AI diagnostics. The available
counter-evidence is instructive: without digital twin augmentation, fault diagnosis accuracy with sparse data falls to
53.9–62.8%, compared with above 95% when complete or DT-augmented datasets are available [28]; cross-machine

7. Challenges and Future Perspectives
This section identifies seven critical challenges impeding the transition from laboratory research to practical
shipboarddeploymentofAI-andDT-enabledintelligentmaintenance,analysesemergingsolutionsforeachchallenge,
and synthesizes current trajectories into a forward-looking roadmap. Table 11 provides a consolidated overview.
7.1. Data Scarcity and Quality
Challenge.High-quality labelled fault data is exceptionally rare in maritime applications. Components are
preventively replaced before failure to satisfy classification society requirements; operators consider operational data
proprietary; and deliberately inducing faults on operational vessels is neither safe nor economically feasible [28].
Without DT augmentation, diagnostic accuracy with sparse fault data drops to 53.9–62.8%, compared with over 95%
under complete datasets [28]. Normal operating data vastly outweighs fault samples in shipboard archives.
Emerging Solutions.(1) Physics-based DTs generate realistic synthetic fault data spanning the full operating
envelope[17].(2)GANvariantssynthesizehigh-fidelityfaultsamplesachievingaccuraciesexceeding96%onseverely
imbalanced datasets. (3) Transfer learning adapts models trained on data-rich aerospace and industrial domains to
marineapplications.(4)Federatedlearningenablesprivacy-preservingcollaborativetrainingacrossfleets[90,91].(5)
Self-supervised pre-training on unlabelled data followed by fine-tuning with minimal labels [86, 116]. (6) DT-based
syntheticdatasetscombiningLSTManomalydetectionwithrootcauseanalysis[117].(7)PHMframeworksemploying
trustworthy DTs with comprehensive uncertainty handling [118].
FutureDirections.Astandardized,publiclyaccessiblemarinemachineryfaultdatabase,analogoustoC-MAPSS
for aerospace, would catalyse the field. Cross-fleet federated DT networks that pool degradation knowledge while
preserving data sovereignty represent a promising architecture. Physics-informed generative models combining the
realism of data-driven generation with physical consistency constraints could produce higher-quality synthetic data
than either approach alone.
7.2. AI Interpretability and Trust (XAI)
Challenge.Deep learning models function as black boxes, producing predictions without explanations. In safety-
critical marine maintenance decisions—where a false negative could lead to catastrophic engine failure at sea, and a
false positive incurs unnecessary cost—opacity undermines trust among marine engineers and classification society
surveyors [93]. Regulatory frameworks increasingly require auditable decision-making processes.
Emerging Solutions.(1) SHAP and LIME provide post-hoc feature importance explanations, with SHAP-based
feature selection reducing dimensionality by approximately 70% while maintaining accuracy [54]. (2) Physics-
InformedAIembedsphysicalconstraintsasinherentlyinterpretablemodelstructure;theKAN-parameterizedphysics
backbone in PM-KMNet [79] explains a substantial fraction of thermal dynamics. (3) Attention visualization in
Transformer models reveals which time segments and sensor channels drive predictions. (4) Knowledge graph-based
causal reasoning [96] provides structured, traceable fault-cause-recommendation paths.
Future Directions.Calibrated explanations with built-in uncertainty intervals are emerging as more stable
alternatives to SHAP and LIME. Human-AI collaborative diagnostic interfaces presenting explanations alongside
predictions, in formats aligned with marine engineers’ mental models, require interdisciplinary research. Regulatory
standardsforAIinterpretabilityinmaritimeclassification,analogoustoFDAguidanceformedicalAI,wouldprovide
a framework for certification.

8. Conclusion
8.1. Summary of Key Findings
This comprehensive review has systematically synthesized 133 publications on AI and Digital Twin enabled
intelligent maintenance for marine machinery spanning 2015 to mid-2026. Several overarching findings emerge.
First, the field is experiencing explosive growth. Within the included corpus, publication volume increased
thirteen-fold from 2015 to 2025, with 2024–2025 accounting for approximately two-thirds of the total corpus
(Supplementary Table S1.5) [28, 6, 34, 35]. This growth is driven by the maritime industry’s digitalization push, the
availability of mature AI and DT technologies from adjacent industries, and the regulatory imperative of autonomous
shipping [31, 32].
Second, AI techniques have progressed through five distinct generations in marine maintenance applications.
Traditional ML (SVM, RF, Bayesian) established the feasibility of data-driven diagnosis with small datasets. Deep
learning(CNN,LSTM,GAN)enabledend-to-endlearningwithautomaticfeatureextractionandnear-ceilingaccuracy
on experimental datasets. Transformer architectures brought global context modelling and superior cross-domain
generalization.FoundationmodelsandLLMspromiseunifiedmulti-taskframeworksforone-stopPHMacrossdiverse
equipment.Physics-InformedAIaddressesthefundamentallimitationsofpurelydata-drivenapproachesbyembedding
physical constraints for consistency, interpretability, and robustness [23, 78].
Third, DT technology has evolved from static three-dimensional models to dynamic, data-driven platforms
integrating physics-based simulation, data-driven surrogate modelling, and hybrid adaptive fusion. The proposed
five-layer Marine Intelligent Maintenance DT Framework provides a unified architecture spanning physical ship
systems through sensor acquisition, DT modelling, and AI prediction to maintenance decision-making, with explicit
accommodation of MASS autonomy requirements.
Fourth, applications across marine diesel engines, bearing and shaft systems, pumps and auxiliary equipment,
and electrical power systems demonstrate that deep learning methods have achieved diagnostic accuracy exceeding
98% under controlled experimental conditions. However, the translation of laboratory performance to real shipboard
deployment remains the central unmet challenge [129, 12].
Fifth, seven critical challenges must be addressed: data scarcity, AI interpretability, cross-vessel generalization,
real-time edge deployment, uncertainty quantification, autonomous maintenance for MASS, and cybersecurity. For
each challenge, promising solutions exist in the research literature but have not yet been integrated into operational
maritime systems.
Complementary advances in time series foundation models [130, 76] and contrastive learning [131, 132] have
expanded the AI toolkit for marine PHM. Stacking-based ensemble methods [133] provide practical baselines.
Acousticemissionmonitoringwithdeeplearning[134]enablesearly-stagesubsurfacedamagedetection.EdgeMLOps
frameworks [29] and cloud-edge-end collaborative architectures [43] address deployment challenges, and shipboard
electrical system condition monitoring rounds out the equipment coverage [48].

8.3. Limitations of This Review
Several limitations should be acknowledged. The review’s language scope (English and Chinese) may have
missed relevant publications in Korean, Japanese, and European languages with strong maritime research traditions.
Reliance on publicly available literature excludes proprietary industrial developments that may be ahead of published
research. The bibliometric analysis, while revealing temporal trends, does not capture citation impact for recent
(2025)publications.ThesearchstrategyomittedScopusandWebofScienceowingtoinstitutionalaccessconstraints;
while ScienceDirect, IEEE Xplore, Springer, and Semantic Scholar provide substantial coverage, some relevant
publications indexed only in those databases may have been missed. Screening and study selection were performed
by a single reviewer rather than through independent dual screening as recommended by PRISMA 2020, and per-
record exclusion reasons at the full-text stage were not retained; selection bias cannot be excluded, although the
systematic search strategy and explicit inclusion criteria provide a transparent and reproducible basis. The rapid pace
of advancement—particularly in foundation models and LLM-based agents—means that the state of the art described
here will continue to evolve rapidly.
8.4. Future Outlook
The trajectory of AI- and DT-enabled intelligent maintenance for marine machinery points clearly toward
increasing autonomy. Across the five AI generations documented in this review, each paradigm shift has reduced
reliance on manual engineering and expanded the scope of what can be predicted, diagnosed, and autonomously
decided. The convergence now underway—foundation models providing multi-task generalization, Physics-Informed
AI ensuring safety-critical reliability, DTs bridging the data-scarcity gap through synthetic data generation, and
edge computing enabling real-time shipboard deployment—will progressively close the gap between laboratory
demonstrations and operational systems.
For marine engineers, this convergence means that vibration-based engine diagnosis is deployable today, battery
SOH monitoring has achieved regulatory acceptance, and DT-based long-term engine prognosis is approaching
production readiness. For AI researchers, the marine domain presents extreme versions of the challenges—data
scarcity, physical constraints, safety-critical deployment, limited connectivity—that define the frontier of robust
and trustworthy AI. For regulators and classification societies, the roadmap from Phases 1–4 provides a structured
framework for technology-neutral certification pathways. For ship operators, the near-term actionable priority is
deployingvibration-basedCNNdiagnosisonexistingsensor-equippedvesselswhileinvestingintheDTinfrastructure
that will enable predictive and autonomous capabilities in the next decade.
If the trajectory documented here continues, within a decade a vessel could depart port with its engine room
diagnosingcomponentwearinrealtime,predictingtheoptimalmaintenancewindowweeksinadvance,andscheduling
repairs autonomously at the next port of call, all without human intervention. Achieving this vision requires sustained
collaborationacrossthemaritimeindustry,theAIresearchcommunity,classificationsocieties,andregulatorybodies.
The technological foundations documented in this review demonstrate that the vision is no longer speculative: it is an
engineering challenge whose pieces are now being assembled.
