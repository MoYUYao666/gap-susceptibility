               Fit with the journal. The manuscript is a study of learned representations in contrastively trained dual-
               encoder neural networks: how the geometry of the joint embedding space that such a network learns does,
               and does not, track its classification behaviour, and how that geometry responds to parameter-efficient
               adaptation (LoRA) and to full fine-tuning. Three contrastive vision–language encoders are analysed

               and directly relevant to neural network learning systems: a controlled intervention on the learned rep-
               resentation (exact global translations of an embedding cluster, together with a perturbation spectrum), a
               first-order analysis of the resulting class-dependent bias field, and an evaluation signal — the Kendall

               ing. Images and captions enter only as inputs to the encoders; no image-processing algorithm is proposed
               or evaluated.

               coefficient of class-relative similarities and not by gap magnitude, including regimes in which the two
               quantities move in opposite directions. A first-order, class-dependent bias field reproduces the measured
               per-class scores to four decimal places (r = 1.000; this is a self-consistency check of the closed form,

               sification while translating the text side does not. We also delineate where the gap remains the more
               informative signal: for retrieval-oriented adaptation it is, while for classification-oriented adaptation τ is,
               giving a task-sensitivity ordering (centroid classification < zero-shot classification < retrieval).

             1. Introduction

                 Contrastive vision-language models map images and texts into a shared embedding space. This space
             exhibits a well-documented modality gap: the image and text clusters are systematically separated (Liang,
             2022). We study the CLIP-style dual encoders (Radford, 2021) in which the gap is measured and has been
             shown to be a structural necessity of contrastive learning (Yi, 2025; Cai, 2026). Because the gap is measurable
             and interpretable, it is widely used as a diagnostic of alignment and retrieval and is often read implicitly as a
             quality proxy: parameter-space objectives target modality imbalance rather than the gap itself (Anon., 2026),


               ∗ Corresponding author.

                Email address: hw4c24@soton.ac.uk (Hongrui Wan)
gap reduction is an explicit objective in method design (Yamaguchi, 2025; Sofer, 2025), gap change predicts
retrieval degradation (Wan, 2026), and gap-aware early stopping has been proposed as a training-time signal
(Wan, 2026). What we dispute is the unconditional form of that reading, not its usefulness for alignment and
retrieval.
     This paper asks a simple question with a surprising answer: is the gap a universal health metric, or only
a task-specific one?
     Our starting observation comes from fine-tuning CLIP ViT-B/32 on Flickr30k. Fine-tuning both encoders
(“both” LoRA, rank 8) increases the Centroid Gap, yet ImageNet zero-shot accuracy stays at 59.7% (50k)—
differing from the frozen model’s 59.3% by only 0.4 percentage points, within sampling noise. The magnitude
of that increase is protocol-dependent, and we report both measurements rather than the larger one. The
training-time logger that ran alongside the original fine-tuning recorded 0.82 → 0.93 (CG∆ = +0.114)
on its own 10% Flickr30k hold-out, whereas the shared evaluator used throughout this paper reads the
same checkpoint file at CG = 0.848 against a frozen 0.815 (CG∆ = +0.033) on Flickr30k rows 2000–3000
(Section 5.10); the two differ because they measure different evaluation subsets, not because the weights
differ. Meanwhile, retrieval R@1 responds to the gap as expected. A variable invisible in gap statistics must
therefore govern zero-shot performance.
     We resolve this decoupling with three controlled experiments, replicated across three model families (CLIP
ViT-B/32, OpenCLIP laion2B, SigLIP). First, gap surgery: exact global translations of the image embedding
cluster along (and orthogonal to) the gap direction. Second, a perturbation spectrum: Gaussian, low-rank,
input-dependent, and class-conditional perturbations at matched magnitudes. Third, a real-checkpoint
dose–response: order-preservation coefficients of five actually fine-tuned models.
     Three findings emerge.

  (i) Among perturbations not aligned with class structure, zero-shot top-1 is governed by τ , the Kendall
      coefficient of class-relative similarity ranks, and is essentially independent of gap magnitude—including
      a regime where shrinking the gap destroys classification.

 (ii) Order preservation is necessary but not sufficient for zero-shot accuracy across mechanisms: among
      perturbations that carry no class information, accuracy cannot survive order destruction and the
      dose–response is monotone within each such family, but class-aligned perturbations improve accuracy
      while lowering the coefficient, because they act on the argmax through a true-class self-field rather than
      by preserving frozen ranks.

 (iii) Tasks fall into a sensitivity spectrum: centroid classification is entirely insensitive, zero-shot classification
      tracks τ , and retrieval tracks the gap.

2. Related Work

    This paper sits at the intersection of several literatures that are usually kept apart: the zero-shot
robustness of contrastive vision–language models (VLMs), the geometry of the modality gap and how
adaptation moves it, catastrophic forgetting under parameter-efficient versus full fine-tuning, the stability of
rank-based evaluation, the calibration of zero-shot classifiers, prompt sensitivity, and the choice of similarity
measure for alignment. We discuss each in turn, stating what the corresponding literature establishes and
how the present study relates to it—where we agree, where we depart, and what we deliberately do not claim.
    The modality gap has been characterized geometrically Liang (2022) and shown to be a structural
necessity of contrastive learning Yi (2025); Cai (2026); its geometry continues to shape downstream analyses
Levi (2025). Gap-based quantities predict retrieval quality, and gap reduction is an explicit objective in
method design Sofer (2025); Yamaguchi (2025); other parameter-space objectives target modality imbalance
rather than the gap itself Anon. (2026), and modality inversion exposes intra-modal misalignment without
closing the gap Mistretta (2025). Closely related in spirit, Chowers (2026) asks whether the gap is a “bug
or a feature,” and reports that moving one modality towards the mean of the other leaves clean accuracy
unchanged; our asymmetry result in Section 5.4 locates that regime: shifts applied to the text side are
benign because they add only a per-image term, while image-side shifts add the destructive per-class bias
field. Grassucci (2026) shows that training-based gap closing leaves instance-wise tasks (retrieval) unaffected
while helping group-wise tasks (clustering); we sharpen this dichotomy by showing that even a ranking-based
instance-wise task—zero-shot classification—is destroyed by naive gap closing, and that how the gap is closed
is decisive. Weight-space interpolation between zero-shot and fine-tuned models similarly preserves both
behaviors Wortsman (2022); our analysis provides the embedding-geometry account. Our prior empirical
study found that the Distribution Gap predicts retrieval R@1 (r = −0.827), motivating gap-aware monitoring
Wan (2026); the present work delineates when such monitoring is appropriate, and when it is not. We show
that order statistics of the class-relative similarity structure—not gap magnitude—are the operative quantity
for zero-shot health under perturbation.


2.1. Zero-shot robustness of contrastive vision–language models

    Contrastive image–text pretraining produces classifiers that transfer zero-shot to unseen datasets, and
CLIP’s original evaluation already showed that this zero-shot accuracy is a function of design choices—prompt
templates, ensembling, and the composition of the pretraining data—rather than of scale alone Radford (2021).
The robustness literature has since established that in-distribution gains do not translate automatically into
out-of-distribution robustness: improvements in accuracy are often not accompanied by corresponding gains
in effective robustness Hendrycks (2021), in-distribution and out-of-distribution accuracy are strongly and
approximately linearly related across many shift datasets Miller (2021), and the distributional robustness of
CLIP-style models is determined primarily by properties of the pretraining data rather than by the training
objective or architecture Fang (2022). Reproducible scaling studies with openly released models have made


                                                       3
such comparisons systematic across data and compute Cherti (2023), while ranking-based compositional
benchmarks show that a substantial part of what looks like robustness in a scalar accuracy disappears once
evaluation requires relational rather than bag-of-words matching Thrush (2022); Yüksekgönül (2023).
    Our study agrees with the premise that zero-shot behaviour is systematic rather than incidental—it is
precisely because it is systematic that we look for the quantity that governs it. We depart in the question
asked: rather than adding another distribution shift to the benchmark, we hold the data fixed and intervene
directly on the embedding geometry, asking which geometric quantity tracks classification health. Because
Fang (2022); Cherti (2023) show that robustness conclusions drawn from a single pretrained model are weak
evidence about the model family, we deliberately replicate across pretrained dual encoders with different
objectives and pretraining corpora. We do not claim that the modality gap is irrelevant to robustness in
general, and we do not claim that our controlled translations reproduce any particular real distribution shift;
the perturbations we construct are probes of geometry, not robustness benchmarks, and nothing here should
be read as a new out-of-distribution result.

2.2. Representation drift and modality-gap geometry under fine-tuning
    The modality gap was established as a genuine property of contrastive VLMs rather than an artefact of
the loss: image and text embeddings occupy systematically separated clusters on the shared sphere, and this
separation persists under a range of training configurations Liang (2022). Subsequent analyses trace the effect
to the functional form of the contrastive objective and the geometry it induces Yi (2025); Cai (2026), and
characterize the anisotropic embedding geometry within which the gap is embedded Levi (2025). In parallel,
the gap has been shown to be causally relevant for some tasks: modality inversion exposes intra-modal
misalignment Mistretta (2025), and training-based gap closing aligns group-wise semantics while leaving
instance-wise tasks such as retrieval largely unaffected Grassucci (2026). Work that moves the text cluster
as a means of manipulating the gap reports weak and inconsistent downstream effects Chowers (2026). On
the adaptation side, a separate literature shows that fine-tuning is not geometry-preserving: adapting a
pretrained backbone can distort its features and underperform a linear probe out of distribution Kumar (2022),
parameter-efficient alternatives such as visual prompt tuning change behaviour at far lower parameter cost
Jia (2022), fine-tuning outcomes are sensitive to protocol details such as initialization Mosbach (2020), and
weight-space interpolation between a zero-shot and a fine-tuned model preserves both behaviours Wortsman
(2022), which is the weight-space counterpart of the embedding-space decoupling we study.
    We agree that the gap is real, geometrically meaningful, and consequential for parts of the pipeline, and
we note that the works above study alignment or retrieval rather than asserting that a small gap implies good
classification; our criticism is directed at the unconditional reading of gap magnitude as a quality proxy, not
at their conclusions. What we add is an intervention rather than a correlation: exact translations of the image
cluster along the estimated gap direction isolate the effect of gap magnitude while leaving the data and the
text side untouched, and in that family gap magnitude and zero-shot accuracy come apart—including regimes
in which shrinking the gap degrades classification. We do not claim that fine-tuning reduces to a global

translation, that feature distortion Kumar (2022) and the class-dependent bias field analysed in Section 4 are
the same phenomenon, or that structure-preserving gap-closing methods Grassucci (2026) are misguided; the
translation is an idealised probe that isolates one component of drift, not a model of any training algorithm.

2.3. Catastrophic forgetting in parameter-efficient versus full fine-tuning

    Catastrophic forgetting—the loss of previously acquired competence after narrow or sequential training—
is a classical property of connectionist models French (1999), and representational studies indicate that much
of it is expressed in hidden-layer representations and depends on the semantic relatedness of the tasks involved
Ramasesh (2021). Regularization-based mitigation such as elastic weight consolidation Kirkpatrick (2017)
and parameter-efficient adaptation such as adapters Houlsby (2019) and low-rank adaptation Anon. (2021)
are the standard tools for trading plasticity against retention. In vision–language models the phenomenon is
documented both for continual supervised fine-tuning of the language component Luo (2025) and, more closely
related to the present setting, for multimodal instruction tuning, which can degrade image-classification
ability relative to the underlying vision encoder even when that encoder is frozen Zhai (2023); prompt-based
domain-incremental learning with CLIP is designed explicitly around this failure mode Nicolas (2024).
    Our checkpoints span exactly the contrast this literature draws—full fine-tuning against low-rank
adaptation—and our observations are qualitatively consistent with it: full fine-tuning damages zero-shot
behaviour far more severely than parameter-efficient adaptation. We depart in what is measured. The
forgetting literature scores retained task competence; we score the preservation of class-relative ordering
under a geometric intervention, and we do not claim to explain, predict, or mitigate forgetting. In particular,
nothing here licenses reading our order-preservation coefficient as a general forgetting metric: it is defined
against a frozen reference model’s ranking, not against held-out task performance, and a model can retain
every task while still having re-ranked its class similarities. Conversely, the evidence that parameter-efficient
methods retain pretrained behaviour better Anon. (2021); Houlsby (2019) constrains our claim: the favourable
behaviour we observe for low-rank adaptation is an instance of a known regularity, not a discovery about
safety in general.

2.4. Ranking stability and order-based evaluation

    The statistics we use have a long history outside vision. Kendall’s coefficient Kendall (1938) and
Spearman’s coefficient Spearman (1904) were introduced as rank-correlation measures precisely because rank
agreement is invariant to monotone rescaling, and ranking is the native evaluation mode of information
retrieval, where the field has also studied the stability of its own conclusions: how sensitive results are to the
sampled set of topics Buckley (2000); Voorhees (2002) and how comparisons across many systems and topics
require multiple-testing correction Carterette (2012). In representation learning the analogous question is
what a similarity score between two representations actually measures—CCA- and SVCCA-based measures
Raghu (2017), centred kernel alignment Kornblith (2019), Procrustes-style shape metrics Williams (2021)
and statistically grounded variants Ding (2021) differ in their invariances and can disagree about the same


                                                        5
pair of models, a point made systematic by a recent survey Klabunde (2025). Vision–language evaluation has
moved in the same direction: ranking-based compositional benchmarks Thrush (2022) and relation-focused
diagnostics Yüksekgönül (2023) show that a single scalar accuracy conceals ordering failures that a per-pair
ranking exposes.
    We adopt this toolkit rather than extend it. Our order-preservation coefficient is an application of an
existing rank statistic, not a new measure, and our contribution is not a metric contribution. What is different
is the use we make of it: as a diagnostic on a model’s own class-relative rankings under a controlled geometric
intervention, rather than as an evaluation of systems against labels. Two consequences for interpretation
follow, and we state them explicitly. First, our coefficient is a preservation statistic relative to a frozen
reference, so it measures drift in ordering, not correctness. Second, because the reference is the frozen model
itself, values are not comparable across different reference models without recalibration. We do not claim
that rank-based statistics dominate magnitude-based similarity measures in general; the lesson of Kornblith
(2019); Ding (2021)—that a similarity number is only as meaningful as the null model against which it is
read—applies to our statistic as much as to any other.


2.5. Calibration of zero-shot classifiers

    Calibration—whether predicted confidence matches empirical accuracy—degrades with depth and with
modern training recipes and is partially repairable by post-hoc rescaling Guo (2017). At scale the in-
distribution picture is more favourable: larger and more accurate models, including zero-shot vision–language
classifiers, are often better calibrated on the distribution they were trained for, while calibration under
distribution shift remains poor even when in-distribution calibration is good Minderer (2021); Ovadia (2019).
Calibration has been studied specifically for vision–language foundation models in zero- and few-shot regimes
Hu (2025), and adaptation is known to degrade the calibration of a zero-shot model relative to the original,
an effect that weight-space interpolation can partly recover Wortsman (2022).
    Our work touches this literature only at a distance, and we make the boundary explicit. Our statistic
is computed from the order of class scores and is therefore invariant to any monotone transformation of
the similarity scale: it carries no information about confidence. A model whose class ordering is perfectly
preserved can still be badly calibrated, and a model whose ordering has been re-ranked can be perfectly
calibrated with respect to its own, different ordering. We consequently make no claim that order preservation
is a calibration measure, that it predicts confidence or uncertainty, or that it is suitable for abstention
and selective prediction. The relationship we establish is between order preservation and top-1 accuracy
under a controlled embedding perturbation; nothing in our analysis extends to probability-level statements.
Conversely, the calibration literature does not predict which geometric perturbations destroy class ordering,
which is the question we ask.

2.6. Prompt sensitivity

    Zero-shot VLM accuracy is acutely sensitive to the text prompt. CLIP’s own protocol relies on
hand-crafted templates and template ensembles, and the choice among them materially changes reported
accuracy Radford (2021). Replacing hand-crafted prompts with learned continuous prompts changes zero-shot
performance substantially Zhou (2022); instance-conditional prompts improve generalization from base to
novel classes Zhou (2022); multi-modal prompting co-adapts visual and textual prompts Khattak (2023); and
test-time prompt tuning adapts the text side per sample Shu (2022). The class text embeddings are thus a
first-class design variable, and small changes to them move accuracy by amounts comparable to the effects we
study.
    Our analysis is consistent with this and gives a geometric reading of part of it: a prompt change re-weights
and shifts the class prototypes, i.e. it acts on the text side, which in our asymmetry analysis contributes only
a benign per-image term at first order, and prompt ensembling averages several such shifts, reducing their
spread. That said, we claim no novel explanation of prompt learning. Learned prompts are optimized against
data and are therefore class-aligned by construction—a regime our class-aligned perturbation family covers
only qualitatively, and not quantitatively, since we contract towards class centroids rather than optimizing a
prompt. Our protocol deliberately fixes a small number of templates and adds an ensemble control so that
prompt variation is not confounded with the geometric intervention; we do not claim template-invariance
of our statistic, and we would not expect values computed under one prompt set to transfer unchanged to
another.


2.7. Alternative similarity measures and metrics for alignment

    Because the modality gap is a centroid distance, the question of which quantity should monitor alignment
is a question about metric choice, and that literature is extensive. Contrastive objectives are commonly
decomposed into alignment and uniformity, two properties that behave very differently and are not inter-
changeable Wang (2020). Between- representation similarity admits many measures—CCA/SVCCA Raghu
(2017), centred kernel alignment Kornblith (2019), shape metrics Williams (2021), statistically tested variants
Ding (2021)—whose invariances differ, as does their verdict on the same pair of models Klabunde (2025); and
even the default cosine similarity can yield arbitrary similarities when the embeddings come from regularized
models Steck (2024). At the task level, retrieval and embedding benchmarks evaluate with rank-based metrics
and show that model rankings depend on the task family and the metric chosen Thakur (2021); Muennighoff
(2023), while work on model convergence uses neighbourhood-overlap alignment between representations
trained on different modalities Huh (2024).
    We read our result as an instance of this general lesson rather than as a rival metric. The modality
gap is a magnitude-based global statistic; the quantity that governs the classification behaviour we study
is a rank-based, reference-relative statistic. That is exactly the task- and metric-dependence documented
above. Accordingly, we do not claim that order preservation is a better alignment metric than CKA, CCA, or



                                                       7
neighbourhood-overlap measures: those quantify the similarity of two representations, whereas ours quantifies
the preservation of an ordering under an intervention, and the two answer different questions. Nor do we claim
that rank-based measures are universally preferable to magnitude-based ones. Our claim is narrower: for
the question “is this classification-oriented adaptation healthy?”, a rank-preservation statistic is informative
where a centroid distance is not, and the reverse holds for retrieval.


2.8. Compositional zero-shot learning

    A closely related but distinct zero-shot setting is compositional zero-shot learning (CZSL), in which the
test classes are unseen attribute–object compositions rather than plain object categories, so that generalisation
requires reasoning over the primitives instead of over the compositions seen in training, and accuracy is
reported on unseen compositions. Three recent works exemplify the class-representation side of that literature:
concept-aware graph convolutional networks, which build a concept graph over the primitives Liu (2025);
semi-negative contrastive subclass discriminative networks, which constrain the contrastive objective at the
subclass level Liu (2026); and multi-level contextual prototype modulation, which modulates prototypes across
levels of context Liu (2025). All three operate on the class/prototype side of the score matrix rather than on
the image-cluster translation studied here. In our decomposition that means they change the frozen quantities
themselves—the per-class mean-text alignment mc and the pairwise margins dij —which is exactly the regime
where our closed form is conditional and must be re-measured rather than extrapolated (Sections 5.4.1
and 5.4.2). Whether the pair-inversion criterion also predicts their failure modes—for instance, whether
subclass-level or prototype-level restructuring promotes compositions that the frozen model ranked below
its top-5—is an open question we flag rather than answer: the criterion is computable for any frozen score
matrix and is therefore testable on CZSL benchmarks, but we have not run it.


2.9. What this paper claims and what it does not

    Taken together, the literatures above agree with parts of our argument and constrain the rest. Because
the claims a reader may reasonably attribute to a paper of this kind are wider than the evidence we present,
we delimit them explicitly.
    What we claim. (i) The modality gap is not a universal health metric for contrastive VLMs: the inference
from gap magnitude to classification health is not warranted unconditionally. We support this by intervention
rather than correlation—exact translations of the image embedding cluster along the gap direction yield
regimes in which the gap shrinks or vanishes while zero-shot accuracy collapses. (ii) Within that translation
family, and among perturbations that are not aligned with class structure, zero-shot top-1 tracks the Kendall
order-preservation coefficient of class-relative similarity ranks and is essentially independent of gap magnitude
(Section 5). (iii) The effect has a first-order mechanism—a class-dependent bias field—which accounts for
the translation response and explains why image-side and text-side translations differ in destructiveness
(Sections 4 and 5.4), and which reconciles the weak text-side effects reported in Chowers (2026). (iv) For



                                                       8
classification-oriented adaptation we recommend monitoring an order statistic alongside, not instead of, the
gap, keeping the gap for retrieval-oriented adaptation Wan (2026); Grassucci (2026).
    What we do not claim. (i) Order preservation is not a sufficient statistic for zero-shot accuracy. The
clearest counterexample is in our own perturbation spectrum: class-conditional contractions improve accuracy
while lowering the coefficient relative to the frozen reference, because they change the argmax without
preserving the frozen ordering. It is a proxy on the destruction side—evidence that an intervention is
destroying class order—and not a predictor of accuracy across all mechanisms. (ii) We do not claim that the
relationship we report holds by definition. The coefficient is computed on rank agreement over the frozen
top-k class sets of a fixed image sample, whereas accuracy is an all-class argmax over the full label set; these
are different functionals, and their agreement is an empirical finding rather than an identity. (iii) We do not
claim a theory of fine-tuning. Global translation is an idealised intervention, not a model of any training
algorithm, and whether a specific adaptation method induces predominantly class-aligned or class-misaligned
drift remains an empirical question for that method. (iv) We do not claim universality of the monitoring
recommendation across tasks, architectures, or data: our evidence concerns classification-oriented evaluation of
dual-encoder VLMs, centroid-based classifiers are insensitive to the intervention by construction, and retrieval
is governed by the gap rather than by the order statistic. (v) We make no claims about calibration, predictive
uncertainty or selective prediction (Section 2.5), about prompt engineering as a method (Section 2.6), about
catastrophic forgetting as measured on task sequences (Section 2.3), or about decoder-based VLMs; where
those literatures are relevant we have said so, but our experiments do not test them. (vi) We do not claim
that the gap is meaningless. For retrieval-oriented adaptation it remains informative Liang (2022); Wan
(2026); Grassucci (2026), and our own results are consistent with that. The thesis we defend is the negative
one—that the gap is not a universal arbiter of representation health—together with a positive replacement
that is explicitly restricted to a task family, an intervention family, and a model family. (vii) We make no
claim about compositional zero-shot learning: our protocol evaluates plain object categories, and Section 2.8
positions the work relative to that literature without testing it.

5. Results

5.1. Gap surgery: classification is not a function of the gap

    Figure 1 shows zero-shot top-1 under the surgery families. Three facts stand out. First, classification
is not invariant to global translation—the naïve “argmax invariance” argument fails because class text
embeddings have non-zero mean and normalization couples directions. Second, the response is strongly
direction-dependent: at λ = 1, the gap-direction shift reduces top-1 to 32.0% while an equal-magnitude
orthogonal shift leaves 42.2%. Third—and most importantly—the relationship between gap and accuracy is
non-monotonic: at λ = 0.5 and λ = 1 the Centroid Gap shrinks to 0.41 and 0.01, yet top-1 drops to 47.6%
and 32.0%. Closing the gap by global translation hurts classification. The centroid contraction family is the
mirror image: it barely moves the gap (0.82 → 0.84 at a = 0.1) and barely moves top-1 (59.3% → 58.8%).
The signature is robust to the prompt: 3-template and 7-template ensembles reproduce parallel collapse
curves (frozen 60.1%/60.3%; λ=1: 36.9%/36.5%).
    Meanwhile, nearest-centroid classification (NCC) is flat at ≈ 69% across all conditions, including full
collapse of zero-shot—a first hint that tasks differ fundamentally in their sensitivity.

5.3. What confers preservation? Not rank, not input-dependence

     The spectrum experiment (Table 1) answers a mechanistic question: which property of a perturbation
preserves class order? Three candidate answers fail. Low rank fails: global rank-k shifts for k = 1, . . . , 64
all yield τ ≈ 0.40–0.44 and top-1 ≈ 38–42%, indistinguishable from Gaussian noise at matched magnitude.
Input-dependence fails: per-image independent low-rank fields are equally destructive (τ ≈ 0.44). What
works is class alignment: contracting each image toward its own class centroid improves accuracy to 78.6%
(a = 0.5), beyond the frozen baseline—and it does so while the coefficient falls to τ = 0.615, below every other
row of the table, including the Gaussian row at τ = 0.761 whose 58.8% we have just called destructive. Class
contraction therefore reaches accuracy by a second, structurally different route rather than by preserving the


quoted here.

frozen ordering, and the correct reading of the table is the weaker one: among perturbations that carry no
class information, order preservation is a necessary condition for accuracy—accuracy cannot survive order
destruction, and the dose–response is monotone within each such family—but it is not a sufficient statistic
across mechanisms (Section 5.3.1).
     Two consequences for the trained-adaptation analogy follow, and we keep them separate. What the
real-checkpoint evidence establishes is a geometric resemblance: unlike low-rank or input-dependent random
fields, the class-aligned family acts on the argmax through the class structure itself, which is also the structure
gradient-driven updates act on. What it does not establish is that order preservation is the mechanism of the
accuracy gain: the true-class self-field of the contraction (+0.0435 ± 0.0288 in mean alignment, Section 5.3.1)
is a separate effect from the permutational re-ranking that τ measures. We therefore make no claim that
preserving τ is what makes a fine-tune healthy; we claim that its erosion is evidence that something is going
wrong, which is the direction in which a monitoring signal is used.

5.3.1. τ is necessary, not sufficient, across intervention families
     Table 1 contains cases in which the lower τ carries the higher accuracy, and we treat them as decisive for
the interpretation of τ rather than as an anomaly. On the same 5,000-image sample, an isotropic Gaussian
field at σ = 0.25 leaves top-1 at 58.8% with τ = 0.761, whereas a class-conditional contraction at a = 0.5
reaches 78.6% with τ = 0.615; the near-equal-τ pair is sharper still (τ = 0.761 at 58.8% against τ = 0.760 at
70.3%). τ is therefore not a sufficient statistic for zero-shot accuracy across mechanisms, and we do not present
it as one. The statement we carry through the paper is narrower: along the destruction direction—that is,
for perturbations that carry no class information—τ is a necessary proxy. Accuracy cannot survive order
destruction, and the class-agnostic dose–response is monotone within every such family: the Gaussian sweep
moves from 58.8% at τ = 0.761 to 41.5% at τ = 0.434 and 15.2% at τ = 0.261.
     The two rows differ in kind, not only in degree. The Gaussian row is a translation with a class-agnostic



                                                                15
field: its only effect on an image is a re-ranking, so its accuracy tracks τ and is never rescued by a favourable
field. The contraction row is not a translation at all—it moves each image toward its own true-class centroid —
and it acts on the argmax through a self-field. With e = µi − x, contraction re-ranks by the exact affine
quantity sc + a⟨e, tc ⟩, whose component along the true class has a strictly positive mean (0.0435 ± 0.0288 over
the sample) and systematically pushes the true class up; its transverse component is zero-mean permutation
noise that re-orders the remaining classes relative to the frozen reference. Contraction therefore changes the
argmax without preserving the frozen ordering: it is a second, structurally different route to accuracy, and
τ —defined against frozen ranks—is not the appropriate reference for it. The same closed form applied to
this different generator reproduces both contraction entries (70.32%/0.7598 at a = 0.25 and 78.58%/0.615 at
a = 0.5); we quote that agreement as a consistency check of the decomposition, in the sense of Section 5.4.3,
and not as independent evidence.


5.3.2. Training-time class alignment hurts zero-shot
    The positive control above (class-conditional contraction, 59.3% → 78.6%) is a perturbation-space
phenomenon, and it does not transfer to a training objective. We fine-tuned pure both-position LoRA (rank
                                                                           
8; only LoRA parameters trainable) with L = LCLIP + λreg 1 − cos(x, cŷ ) , where ŷ is the frozen model’s
pseudo-label and cŷ the (EMA-tracked or fixed) ImageNet class centroid. The response is monotone and
opposite to the post-hoc effect: as λreg grows from 0 to 1 (three seeds at λreg ∈ {0, 0.1, 0.5, 1}; the intermediate
points single-seed), zero-shot top-1 falls from 58.11% ± 0.05 to 55.60% ± 0.27 and τ from 0.708 ± 0.006 to
0.635 ± 0.008, while Flickr30k validation loss improves from 2.33 to 2.12. Training-time class alignment
therefore trades zero-shot order preservation for in-distribution fit—the opposite of post-hoc contraction,
which leaves the (true-label) class ordering usable while increasing the true-class margin. Two factors explain
the difference: pseudo-labels are wrong on ≈ 41% of images, and gradient-driven alignment re-ranks classes
during optimization, which is what the bias-field analysis of Section 4 predicts once the perturbation is
generated by an update rather than fixed in advance. Post-hoc class alignment helps; training-time class
alignment hurts—a sharper boundary for the findings above, and one that also limits how far the class-aligned
family can be read as a model of what fine-tuning does.


5.4. Mechanism: the class bias field

    The analysis of Section 4 makes two quantitative predictions, both verified. First, the per-class score
shift under a gap-direction translation should equal λ∥g∥ mc : the measured mean per-class shifts at λ = 1
match the prediction with Pearson r = 1.000 (std(mc ) = 0.067). This agreement is a self-consistency check,
not independent validation: both sides are functions of the same frozen scores and margins, and with the
margins frozen the closed form is an identity rather than an approximation (Sections 5.4.3 and 5.4.2), so the
two curves must agree. What the check does establish is that our implementation of the closed form and a
separate Kendall re-computation of the shifted scores coincide to within rounding (0.0019 maximum, 0.0013
mean over the nine λ values), and that the per-class profile has the predicted shape. Independent evidence for


                                                        16
the relation is elsewhere: a held-out model family (Section 5.5.1) and a disjoint 50,000-image sample, where
the closed form calibrated on 5,000 images differs from the measurement by at most 0.0011. The re-ranking
that destroys zero-shot accuracy is thus accounted for by a fixed, class-dependent bias field, and we do not
claim that this mechanism is the only one available—class-conditional contraction is a second, structurally
different route to accuracy that the bias field also predicts, but with a different generator (Section 5.3.1).
Second, the bias field predicts an asymmetry between translating the image side and translating the text
side: a text-side translation adds the per-image term δ · x (zero mean across images, since image embeddings
are nearly centered), rather than a per-class bias, and should therefore be markedly less destructive. This is
exactly what we observe (Table 2): at λ = 1 the image-side shift reduces top-1 to 31.9% while the symmetric
text-side shift leaves 45.1%; at λ = 2 top-1 falls to 13.5% vs. 38.3%. The asymmetry also reconciles the
seemingly conflicting literature: prior work that moves one modality towards the mean of the other reports
that decreasing the gap does not change clean accuracy (Chowers, 2026); in our protocol that is the text-side
regime, where the shift adds only the benign per-image term.

negative—using no free parameters and no distributional assumption on m; the maximum absolute difference
is 0.0019 and the mean 0.0013. The τ –top-1 relationship of Section 5 is thus not merely correlational: the
order destruction of any image under any gap-direction translation is computable in closed form from the
frozen model. Section 5.4.3 states precisely what that agreement does and does not establish.

5.4.2. The inversion criterion is exact, not a first-order expansion
     The analysis of Section 4 is written as a first-order expansion, which may suggest a small-perturbation
assumption that our experiments violate, since the perturbations used are of size ≈ ∥g∥. That premise does
not apply to the criterion we actually use. The pair-inversion statement of Section 5.4.1—pair (i, j) inverts if
and only if λ∥g∥(mj − mi ) > dij —is an exact equivalence for every λ, positive or negative, once the margins
                                                      ˆ
are frozen. Writing x̃ = (x + δ)/∥x + δ∥ with δ = λ∥g∥d,

                                         s̃c = x̃ · tc = (sc + λ∥g∥mc )/∥x + δ∥,

and the denominator is a strictly positive per-image constant, common to all classes: it cannot change any
pairwise comparison, at any λ and to any order in ∥δ∥. The criterion therefore contains no truncated term at
all; it is the joint event “small margin × large bias difference”, evaluated on frozen quantities. Nothing in
Table 3 assumes λ∥g∥ ≪ 1, and its inputs ({mc }, {dij }, the frozen top-5 sets) are read off the frozen model.
The agreement over nine λ values spanning λ = −2 to λ = +4—where the translation reaches four times the
gap magnitude—is what a non-perturbative criterion should produce.
     First-order language does occur in the paper, but only where a genuinely truncated quantity is being
interpreted: (i) the text-side “per-class-constant” discussion, where a shift applied to tc is renormalised per
class and therefore does not cancel in a pairwise comparison; and (ii) statements about score magnitudes
(the per-class profile λ∥g∥mc and the Centroid-Gap displacement). The per-class shift agreement quoted


                                                             18
in Section 5.4 (r = 1.000 against λ∥g∥mc , with std(mc ) = 0.0673) belongs to this second class, not to the
ordering result: it is a first-order magnitude statement, taken as a mean over images, and we label it as such
rather than presenting it as independent validation. In the ordering analysis, higher-order terms in ∥δ∥ are
not neglected but absent: they enter only through changes in the frozen quantities themselves (mc , {dij },
and the membership of the top-5 sets), i.e. exactly in the regimes where the closed form does not claim to
hold—text-side surgery, class-conditional contraction (an isomorphic bias field with a different generator),
and any training-time update. Where those quantities change, we re-measure instead of extrapolating
(Sections 5.3.2, 5.6.1, 5.6.2).
     Two residuals make the point quantitatively. Against the measured curve on the same 5,000-image
sample the exact predictor differs by at most 0.0019 over the nine λ values (mean 0.0013); against the disjoint
50,000-image sample it differs by at most 0.0011 (mean 0.0009) over the three λ values where the two grids
overlap. Residuals at this level are threshold and tie handling between the pair-count and the Kendall
implementations—not evidence of a truncated expansion. For contrast, the first step that does introduce an
approximation—replacing the frozen margins with a Gaussian surrogate—costs far more: Pearson 0.9865
with maximum error 0.1695 (Section 5.5.1). The claim we make is the narrow one: the criterion is exact for
any λ under frozen margins, and the first-order expansion is a device for interpreting the bias field, not a
precondition for the result.

5.4.3. The r = 1.000 agreement is a self-consistency check, not independent validation
     A correlation of exactly 1.000 between predicted and measured τ is anomalous as evidence, and we do
not present it as validation. Both sides of that comparison are functions of the same frozen scores: the
prediction reads {mc } and {dij } off the frozen model, and the “measured” τ applies the exact translation
to those same frozen embeddings and recomputes Kendall over the same frozen top-5 sets. Given frozen
margins the inversion condition is an equivalence rather than an approximation (Section 5.4.2), so the two
curves must agree; what the 1.000 reports is that our implementation of the closed form and an independent
Kendall re-computation of the shifted scores coincide to within rounding—the two sides are not the same
array (identical = false; maximum absolute difference 0.0019, mean 0.0013 over the nine λ values). We
therefore label it a self-consistency check of the implementation, report it with the residual rather than the
correlation alone, and state explicitly that it is not evidence of generalisation.
     The independent evidence is elsewhere, and we quote its weaker form: (i) a cross-family hold-out,
in which a τ -based predictor fitted on the other families retains positive held-out R2 for CLIP (0.371,
ntest = 20) and SigLIP (0.378, ntest = 4) and ranks all 28 conditions correctly overall (pooled Spearman 0.927,
p = 1.4 × 10−12 ), while a Centroid-Gap predictor is worse than the mean in every fold (R2 = −1.13 to −1.52;
pooled Spearman −0.251, p = 0.20); and (ii) a new-sample check, in which the closed form calibrated on
the 5,000-image subsample predicts τ measured on the disjoint 50,000-image set at the three λ values where
the grids overlap, with maximum absolute difference 0.0011 (mean 0.0009). We also keep the failure case in
view: substituting a Gaussian surrogate for the margin distribution—the first step at which a distributional


                                                       19
assumption enters—gives Pearson 0.9865 and maximum error 0.1695, against 0.0019 for the frozen-margin
closure.
    Preconditions, stated so that the check cannot be over-read. The identity requires all four of: frozen
text embeddings and frozen margins on both sides; an image-side translation (which is what makes the
renormalisation a common positive factor); the same frozen top-5 sets on both sides; and no distributional
assumption on the margins. It holds for any λ, not only small ones. Remove any one of the four—as text-side
surgery, class-conditional contraction, or any training-time update does—and the check no longer applies.


5.5. Scope of the order-preservation statistic

    The four analyses that follow fix how a τ number may be read: what it generalises to, what it is not, at
which k it is defined, and how much of it is seed noise.


5.5.1. Out-of-family generalization
    The claim that τ governs zero-shot accuracy would be circular if τ were merely a restatement of it.
We therefore held out an entire model family and fitted the predictor on the remaining families: across the
28 archived conditions (CLIP 20, OpenCLIP 4, SigLIP 4), a τ -based predictor retains positive held-out
R2 for CLIP (0.371) and SigLIP (0.378) and ranks all conditions correctly overall (Spearman ρ = 0.927,
p = 1.4 × 10−12 ), whereas a Centroid-Gap-based predictor is worse than the mean in every fold (R2 = −1.13
to −1.52; overall ρ = −0.251, p = 0.20). The mapping is monotone but family-calibrated, so we state ordering
across families rather than absolute prediction. Two caveats belong with these numbers: the folds are very
unequal (ntest = 20 for CLIP against 4 for OpenCLIP and SigLIP), so the negative OpenCLIP R2 is a
small-sample artefact rather than a failure of the predictor, and the pooled Spearman (0.927, n = 28) is the
primary summary. The exact pair-inversion predictor is likewise not a universal law: a Gaussian surrogate
for the margin distribution reaches only Pearson r = 0.987 (r2 = 0.973) with maximum error 0.170 on the
same nine λ values, so the closed form is accurate where the frozen margins are used directly and degrades
once the margin distribution is modelled.


5.5.2. τ and accuracy are not the same object
    It is natural to ask whether the τ –accuracy relation holds partly by definition, since zero-shot prediction
is an argmax over similarity ranks. The two quantities are computed on different objects, and our own tables
show it. τ is the mean Kendall coefficient between frozen and perturbed ranks within each image’s frozen
top-5 set (5 of the 1,000 ImageNet classes), while accuracy is a full 1,000-way argmax. A statistic over a
frozen 5-class neighbourhood and a 1,000-way decision cannot be the same quantity, and the counterexample
of Section 5.3.1 (τ = 0.615 with 78.6%, τ = 0.761 with 58.8%) is a direct demonstration that neither one
implies the other across mechanisms. Two further facts make the relation informative rather than tautological:
the closed form of Section 5.4.1 computes τ (λ) from frozen margins with no free parameters (maximum error
0.0019), and a τ -based predictor fitted on the other model families still has positive held-out R2 on a family


                                                      20
it has never seen (0.371 for CLIP, 0.378 for SigLIP; pooled Spearman 0.927), which a restatement of accuracy
could not achieve.
      The limitation this implies must be stated as plainly as the claim. Because τ is evaluated on the frozen
top-5 set, it is blind to damage caused from outside that set: a perturbation may promote a class that
the frozen model ranked sixth (or lower) without changing any of the ten in-set pairwise comparisons, and
τ will not move. The exact top-1 predictor inherits the same restriction—it counts only the four in-set
competitors of the frozen top-1—so it is a lower bound on the true re-ranking rate rather than an estimate of
full 1,000-way accuracy. τ is thus a local order-preservation statistic: it measures order preservation inside
the frozen neighbourhood on which it is computed, and it proxies accuracy only for perturbations whose
action on the score vector is a per-class shift (Section 5.4.2)—which is precisely the bias-field regime, and not
a universal property of embedding drift.

5.6. τ as a training-time signal

    The dose–response in Figure 2 is static; Figure 3 shows that the same decoupling unfolds during
training. Retraining both-position LoRA while logging per-epoch weights (same documented configuration;
the Centroid Gap is measured on the Flickr30k validation subset), the gap jumps in the first epoch then
settles (0.815 → 0.848; this is the same 0.848 the shared evaluator reads on the archived checkpoint, while that
run’s own training-time logger reported 0.93, a 0.085 protocol gap on identical weights that we report rather
than resolve—Section 5.10), while τ declines early (1.00 → 0.75 by epoch 3) then plateaus and zero-shot top-1
remains flat at 58.5–58.6% throughout. Text-only training leaves CG and top-1 essentially unchanged but
still erodes τ modestly (1.00 → 0.79), consistent with text prototypes drifting as a block while the visual
ordering stays intact. Three practical points follow, and we separate what we measured from what we did not.
First, τ is computable from the top-k class sets a validation pass already produces, so it costs no additional
forward pass. Second, within a single training trajectory it tracks zero-shot accuracy closely: over the ten
runs we evaluated epoch by epoch, τ rises and falls with top-1 at a mean Pearson correlation of 0.90 (10/10
runs positive; Section 5.12). Third, on the three runs for which we also have per-epoch validation loss, the
ranking ability of τ is the more reliable of the two: Spearman against zero-shot top-1 is +0.95, +0.81 and
+0.98 for τ (Fisher mean +0.938, positive in all three), against +0.88, −0.78 and −0.90 for the validation
loss (Fisher mean −0.371), whose minimum arrives at epochs where zero-shot accuracy is already the lower
one. On the same runs τ falls by 6.29% relative to its first epoch while top-1 falls 2.86% and the validation
loss rises 1.11%, and τ first declines no later than the validation loss in 3 of 3 runs but earlier than top-1 in 0
of 3. What these runs do not establish is a better stopping decision: all three trajectories are best at their
first epoch, so τ , the validation loss, top-1 and a constant rule all select it, and the mean selected top-1 is
59.66% for τ and the oracle alike, against 58.66% for validation-loss selection. What this does not license
is a stopping rule. Evaluating four stopping criteria on the same ten runs, halting where τ peaks selects
checkpoints whose mean top-1 is 59.44%—identical to the 59.44% obtained by ignoring every signal and


                                                          23
halting after the first epoch, and below the 59.49% of an oracle that sees the ImageNet labels. τ is therefore
a diagnostic of order erosion rather than a decision rule, and we report it as such. One qualification belongs
here rather than in a footnote, because it was measured later and it constrains the claim: “top-1 stays flat” is
protocol-dependent. Under the strict LoRA-only re-run of Section 5.6.2, top-1 does decline (59.79 → 58.08%)
alongside τ (0.7563 → 0.7088), and all three monitoring signals degrade there—τ most sensitively in relative
terms, which is the reading we carry forward.

5.6.2. Protocol note: pure-LoRA re-verification
    The retrain checkpoints of Section 5.6 archive the LoRA adapter weights only. The strict re-evaluation
is therefore the one that matters: the dose–response script selects trainable parameters by name (the lora_
prefix), so it cannot update embeddings or LayerNorms. That run gives τ = 0.7563 at epoch 1, 0.7270 at
epoch 3 and 0.7088 at epoch 10, with top-1 59.79 → 58.08% (Table 6). An independent re-evaluation of the
archived submitted checkpoints (both-position LoRA, LoRA-component reconstruction, same 5,000-image
sample) gives τ = 0.764 at epoch 1 and 0.750 at epoch 10, with top-1 flat at 58.58–58.62%. The two protocols
therefore agree on the τ level (0.71–0.76) and on its early erosion, but they differ in one respect we do not
paper over: top-1 is flat in the re-evaluated archived run and declines in the strict re-run, so the “top-1 is inert”
reading is protocol-dependent rather than universal. The conclusion this section supports is correspondingly
the narrower one: all three monitoring signals degrade under fine-tuning, and τ degrades fastest in relative
terms, which is what makes it the more sensitive of the three rather than the only one that moves.


5.6.3. Two limitations disclosed after internal review
    Verifying Sections 5.6.1 and 5.6.2 against their raw records produced two corrections, which we state
here rather than leave implicit.
    (1) No monitoring signal is inert, and τ is not the only one that moves. An earlier reading—that
τ alone moves under fine-tuning while top-1 and validation loss stay put—is not supported by the strict
LoRA-only sweep. Over epochs 1 → 10 of that run, τ falls 0.7563 → 0.7088, top-1 falls 59.79 → 58.08%, and
validation loss reaches its minimum at epoch 3 (2.3101) before worsening (2.3591 at the endpoint, Table 6).
All three signals therefore degrade; what distinguishes τ is that it erodes fastest in relative terms (6.3%
against 2.9% for top-1). The defensible claim is “τ is the more sensitive of the three”, not “τ is the only signal
that carries information”. No other section of this paper asserts the stronger reading.


                                                         25
     (2) The “top-1 is inert” reading is protocol-dependent. Under the re-evaluation of the archived
submitted checkpoints (LoRA-component reconstruction, same 5,000-image sample), top-1 stays inside
58.50–58.62% across the ten epochs while τ falls 0.7638 → 0.7497: there, top-1 really is flat (epochs 1–4 alone
occupy the narrower band 58.58–58.62% quoted in Section 5.6.2). Under the strict LoRA-only re-run, in
which the trainable parameters are selected by name (the lora_ prefix), top-1 falls 59.79 → 58.08% alongside
τ (0.7563 → 0.7088). The two protocols agree on the τ level and on its early erosion, and they disagree on

re-run and how the archived checkpoints are reconstructed), not a data-selection choice, and we report both

5.8.2. The signature vanishes where the bias field does: DINOv2
    The theory predicts that the destructive part of a translation is λ∥g∥ mc with per-class spread set by the
dispersion of mc = dˆ · cc , where cc is the class c prototype—the analogue of the text embedding tc in a model
that has no text tower. In a non-contrastive vision model (DINOv2-base, class prototypes as the reference
cluster), the prototype–image gap is tiny (CG = 0.043, nineteen times smaller than CLIP’s), so the bias field
is nearly constant across classes. The signature should therefore vanish—and it does: under the same surgery
grid and in both variants (gap-direction and orthogonal, magnitude-matched), τ ≥ 0.930 at every λ including
λ = 4 (0.930–0.995 for the gap direction, 0.950–0.997 for the orthogonal one) and nearest-prototype top-1
stays inside 48.18–48.26%, even where CG shrinks to 0.001. Order destruction is governed by the dispersion
of mc , not by the architecture class: CLIP-class models have large dispersion and collapse, DINOv2 has
none and is immune. This negative control draws the boundary of our claims and is a further point-wise
confirmation of the bias-field model.

5.11. Five hardening experiments: the limits of the monitoring claim

    Five hardening experiments were run after those of Sections 5.5.3–5.10: prompt-template sensitivity,
bootstrap confidence intervals together with a k-sweep, two further seeds for each non-Flickr30k dataset, and



                                                       31
a decomposition of the real-checkpoint offset. None of them reverses a qualitative conclusion of this paper;
each of them constrains how a τ number may be quoted.
     The absolute level of τ is template-conditional. Every reported number in this paper uses the
single template a photo of a {}, and that convention was previously cited as a caveat rather than measured.
We re-ran the freezing/surgery protocol under five templates (a photo of a {}, a photo of the {}, a
photo of a {} in the wild, {}, a close-up photo of a {}) on ImageNet-5k, DTD and Food-101, with
τ computed on each template’s own frozen top-5 sets, so that every τ is template-internal and comparable
only within a template column (Table 11). The reassuring half first: the collapse signature is template-stable.
τ falls monotonically as λ goes 0 → 0.5 → 1 → 2 in 15 of 15 benchmark×template combinations, τ (2) lies in
[0.071, 0.297] throughout, and the default template is the best of the five on ImageNet-5k (59.10% frozen top-1,
the maximum) and on DTD (43.14%, the maximum) and second of five on Food-101 (82.06%, behind a photo
of the {} at 83.47%), so the published numbers are not the product of an unusually favourable prompt.
The consequential half: the absolute level is not stable. The spread of τ across the five templates reaches
0.111 (Food-101, λ = 1: 0.3740–0.4849) and, for λ ≥ 0.5, never falls below 0.065. Each of these spreads is 4.3
to 28.4 times the corresponding bootstrap half-width of the cell it belongs to (the resolution measurement
below), and the largest is about eight times the largest seed standard deviation of the three-seed comparison
below. The frozen class-relative order is itself largely template-specific: with no intervention at all, τ between
the default template and {} is 0.565 on ImageNet-5k and 0.481 on DTD, and the two templates share only
57.1% of their frozen top-5 sets on ImageNet-5k (Jaccard). Both gap variants reported in the source (the gap
re-estimated per template, and the default template’s gap reused) give the same spreads to within 0.0011.
The operational consequence is that a τ value quoted without its template carries an uncertainty of the same
order as this spread, so any τ level used as a monitoring tripwire is template-conditional and must be quoted
with the template that produced it.

     A fixed τ level is a change detector, not a health verdict. The specificity problem is concrete. On
the probing grid the τ < 0.5 alarm fires first at λ = +1.0 when τ is measured at k = 2, at λ = +0.5 at k = 3,
and already at λ = −0.5 for every k ≥ 5. At this paper’s operating point (k = 5) the two smallest probes are
0.4931 and 0.4656 at λ = −0.5 and λ = +0.5: the alarm is tripped at the first non-zero magnitude in both
directions, so a single level separates neither severity nor direction. The λ = −0.5 probe is a gap-widening
intervention, not a gap-closing one—the Centroid Gap rises from 0.8154 to 1.0729, the opposite sign to the
surgery the collapse signature is about—and its 5k top-1 falls from 59.10% to 29.48%. On ImageNet-5k the
alarm is therefore not a false positive in the accuracy sense; the error it invites is interpretive, because the
same τ excursion is produced by a sign-flipped bias field that a “gap health” reading would misattribute
to gap closure. The one point on record where the same rule fires on a marginally accuracy-improving
intervention is the rare-species benchmark, where λ = −0.5 gives 17.00% against a frozen 16.40% while
τ = 0.428 < 0.5; that benchmark carries 1,000 images and its +0.6-point difference was not tested against
a bootstrap interval, so we record it as a caveat rather than a demonstration. What follows for practice is
the reading we adopt throughout: a τ level detects change, and must be paired with a direction check—the
sign of the gap movement, or the identity of the perturbation family being evaluated—before it is read as a
verdict on health.

the initialisation and the shuffle order, which makes its τ5 spread a clean estimate of that sensitivity (COCO
and Food-101 resample 2,000 pairs, so their seeds vary the data as well).
     The real-checkpoint offset remains an open question. Section 5.10 records that every controlled

curve). It is not explained by the geometry the surgery protocol leaves out. All embeddings are unit-norm
and logit_scale is identically 100.0 for the frozen model and for all thirteen checkpoints of the offset

family occupies.

5.12. Within-dataset evidence: ten runs evaluated epoch by epoch
     Every comparison above pools runs drawn from different datasets, which makes the coefficient hard to

6. Discussion

     When is the gap a health metric? Our results delineate the applicability boundary of gap-based
monitoring (Wan, 2026): for retrieval-oriented adaptation, the gap (and its distributional extensions) is the
right signal; for classification-oriented adaptation in the settings we test, order preservation τ is the more
informative of the two signals; centroid-based pipelines need neither. Mixed-purpose practitioners should
monitor both, or derive τ cheaply from top-k class sets already computed during validation.


                                                               36
    Why does trained fine-tuning preserve order? Two observations, one established and one open.
Established: LoRA updates are gradient-aligned with the task’s class structure, so the induced embedding
perturbation field is at least partly class-aligned by construction—the class-aligned family of our spectrum—
and measured real fine-tunes displace the image cluster almost entirely orthogonally to the gap direction
(86.0–99.7% of the displacement, against 0% by construction for synthetic gap surgery). That separates
the gap component from the order-destroying component of a perturbation: both-position LoRA grows the
gap while τ stays high, which is the decoupling this paper is about. Open: the same real checkpoints sit
+3.62 ± 1.41 accuracy points above the synthetic τ →top-1 curve, and we could not identify a measured
geometry statistic that accounts for more than half of that offset. We therefore treat the vertical offset as an
outstanding question and the orthogonal character of the displacement as its leading candidate explanation,
not as the mechanism (Section 5.11).
    Limitations. We state the boundary of our claim in the same terms as the claim, and every item
below is measured rather than conceded in principle. (a) Order preservation is necessary, not sufficient. Our
own spectrum contains the counterexample: class-conditional contraction reaches 78.6% at τ = 0.615 while
Gaussian noise with the same magnitude sits at 58.8% at τ = 0.761. Contraction is a second, structurally
different route to accuracy—a true-class self-field with mean +0.0435 ± 0.0288 plus a zero-mean permutation
noise—so τ is informative on the destruction side and not a predictor of accuracy across all mechanisms
(Section 5.3.1). (b) The absolute level of τ is not a portable number. It is k-dependent (at λ = 0.5 it falls
from 0.496 at k = 3 to 0.411 at k = 100, and the level at λ = 1 shifts by 0.104 across k ∈ {2, . . . , 100})
and template-dependent (cross-template spread 0.065–0.111 for λ ≥ 0.5, against bootstrap half-widths of
0.003–0.031; with no intervention at all, the frozen order of the default template correlates only 0.565 with
a bare class-name template on ImageNet-5k and 0.481 on DTD). A τ level is therefore comparable only
within a fixed (k, template) cell, and any tripwire value must be quoted with both (Sections 5.5.3 and 5.11).
(c) τ orders datasets, not individual runs. The within-dataset seed correlations are −0.5 (COCO), −0.5
(Food-101) and +1.0 (DTD), and COCO seed 42 has the lowest τ5 of its three seeds while having the highest
accuracy; the cross-dataset contrast is +0.85 over nine runs against −0.27 for the gap, but it does not license
reading τ as a per-run ranker inside a high-τ band. (d) Real checkpoints sit above the synthetic curve and we
cannot explain why. Measured over twelve vision-LoRA checkpoints the excess is +3.62 ± 1.41 accuracy points
at matched τ ; unit norms and a constant logit_scale exclude norms and temperature by construction,
within-class spread and top-1 margins move one to two orders of magnitude less than the embeddings do,
and the best single correlate (fraction of top-1 predictions changed, Pearson 0.689) accounts for less than half
the variance. We name the leading candidate—86.0–99.7% of the real displacement is orthogonal to the gap
direction, a region the one-parameter synthetic family cannot enter—and state the offset as open rather than
modelled (Section 5.11). (e) Global translation is a crude model of real embedding drift, an idealised probe
rather than a model of any training algorithm, and τ is measured against frozen ranks, which is informative
but not a task-optimal reference. (f ) The full fine-tuning evidence is reconstruction-only: the original script



                                                      37
is lost and the seed-42 reconstruction diverges, so those three rows are reported as reconstructions rather
than as archived weights (Section 3). (g) Extension to decoder-based VLMs is tested in one model only, with
a first-token protocol that underestimates absolute accuracy (Section 5.8.1).


7. Conclusion

    The modality gap is not a universal health metric. Under controlled surgery—34 perturbation conditions
across three model families, plus seventeen fine-tuned checkpoints—zero-shot classification accuracy is governed
by the order-preservation coefficient τ and is essentially uncorrelated with gap magnitude, including regimes
where the two move in opposite directions. Class-aligned perturbations, the kind trained adaptation produces,
raise accuracy while lowering τ ; low-rank and input-dependent random fields only destroy it. In the settings
tested here we find τ to be the more informative monitoring signal for classification-oriented fine-tuning,
and reserve the gap for retrieval. Because the cross-family mapping is family-calibrated (R2 0.371/0.378
with pooled Spearman 0.927), we recommend τ as an ordering proxy rather than as a calibrated accuracy
predictor, and we recommend it in the narrow form the evidence supports: a τ that erodes is evidence of
damage, quoted with the k and the prompt template that produced it, read alongside the gap rather than
instead of it, and not applied to rank individual runs within one dataset (Section 5.11). Code and data:
github.com/MoYUYao666/gap-susceptibility (reproducibility archive upon publication).
