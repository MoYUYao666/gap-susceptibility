Abstract:

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) of vision-language models is widespread, yet
how fine-tuning affects cross-modal alignment remains poorly understood. Through
116 experiments spanning two PEFT methods, three model architectures, two retrieval
datasets, and three zero-shot benchmarks, we conduct the first systematic study of
modality gap evolution during fine-tuning. We find that text-only fine-tuning
overwhelmingly preserves cross-modal alignment better than joint modality fine-tuning
across LoRA, Adapter, BLIP-1, and SigLIP configurations. Full fine-tuning increases
the Centroid Gap by 24% and destroys zero-shot accuracy by 53.3%, while text-only
LoRA limits the gap increase to +0.079 and zero-shot degradation to 1.7%. We further
find that Text+Vis-L1 achieves frozen-level alignment preservation with competitive
retrieval. We propose the Visual Fragility Interpretation---visual embedding spaces
appear more sensitive to parameter perturbation than text spaces---as a parsimonious
account, and provide direct causal evidence through noise sensitivity, gradient
analysis, and effective rank tracking. Building on our finding that the Distribution Gap
predicts retrieval performance at r=-0.827 (partial r=-0.807 controlling for Centroid
Gap), we introduce DG-based Alignment Monitoring, with DG-Guided Early Stopping
as a lightweight zero-overhead instantiation. Our results provide systematic empirical
grounding for gap-aware fine-tuning: protect the visual encoder, fine-tune text, and
monitor DG.

1. Introduction
Vision-language models (VLMs) pre-trained with contrastive objectives, such as
CLIP [1], have become foundational building blocks for a wide range of downstream
tasks including image-text retrieval, zero-shot classification, and multimodal generation. These models learn a joint embedding space where semantically related images
and texts are mapped to nearby points. However, this embedding space exhibits a
well-documented phenomenon known as the modality gap—a systematic separation
between the clusters of image embeddings and text embeddings [2]. The modality gap
has been shown to be a structural consequence of the contrastive learning objective
rather than an artifact of initialization or data mismatch [3, 4], and its properties have
been analyzed through the lens of embedding geometry [5, 6].
In practice, CLIP and similar models are rarely used in their pre-trained form.
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods, particularly Low-Rank Adaptation
(LoRA) [7], have become the dominant approach for adapting VLMs to downstream
tasks due to their minimal parameter overhead and avoidance of catastrophic forgetting. Recent work has revealed that naive application of LoRA to multimodal tasks introduces a rank allocation imbalance favoring the text modality [8], and that modalityspecific parameter decomposition can improve alignment [9]. However, these works
focus on how to fix the imbalance through more sophisticated allocation strategies,
rather than understanding why the imbalance occurs and what it reveals about the nature of cross-modal alignment.
In this paper, we take a different approach. Rather than designing a new PEFT
method, we conduct a systematic empirical characterization of how parameter-efficient
fine-tuning reshapes cross-modal representation geometry. While prior work [10] introduced the CG/DG decomposition and characterized their static properties on pretrained models, we provide the first systematic study of how these metrics evolve
dynamically during fine-tuning—revealing non-monotonic interactions between PEFT
configuration choices and gap evolution that are invisible in static analysis alone. We

2

further provide direct causal evidence for the observed regularities through parameter
noise sensitivity analysis, gradient contribution measurement, and effective rank tracking. Our central question is: how does the modality gap evolve during PEFT, and
what does this evolution reveal about effective fine-tuning strategies? This question
can be framed as a pattern recognition problem in optimization dynamics—identifying
systematic regularities in how training choices (which modality, which method, which
rank) map to alignment outcomes across architectures, datasets, and objectives. To answer this, we conduct 116 experiments measuring Centroid Gap (CG) and Distribution
Gap (DG) across LoRA rank sweeps, position variants (text-only, vision-only, both),
scaling factor (α) ablations, target module configurations, and multiple PEFT methods
(LoRA, Adapter). We extend our analysis across four models (CLIP ViT-B/32, ViTL/14, BLIP-1, SigLIP), two datasets (Flickr30k, MS-COCO), three zero-shot benchmarks (CIFAR-100, DTD, Food101), and provide statistical validation through 5-seed
experiments.
Our investigation yields several key findings:
1. Full fine-tuning catastrophically disrupts cross-modal alignment, increasing
CG by 24% on Flickr30k and 18% on COCO, with 5-seed statistical significance
(p < 0.001).
2. Text-only adaptation overwhelmingly yields the smallest alignment degradation among evaluated configurations: on LoRA (CG∆: −0.064 vs. −0.032 for
both), Adapter (−0.046 vs. −0.040), BLIP-1 (+0.047 vs. +0.152), and ViT-L
(−0.036 vs. +0.128 for rank-4). On COCO, both-position achieves a marginally
better CG∆ (−0.052 vs. −0.041), the sole exception across 4 dimensions of crossvalidation.
3. The Distribution Gap is a significantly stronger predictor of retrieval performance than the Centroid Gap, achieving a Pearson correlation of r = −0.827
with R@1 (vs. r = −0.683 for CG, n = 81), and maintaining r = −0.807 in
partial correlation analysis controlling for CG.
4. LoRA rank and Adapter dimension have opposite effects on gap preservation:
lower LoRA rank yields smaller alignment degradation (rank-4 CG∆: −0.027 vs.

3

rank-64: +0.012), while larger Adapter bottleneck dimensions reduce the gap
more effectively (d=64 CG∆: −0.217 vs. d=4: −0.069), revealing a fundamental
difference between hard low-rank constraints and soft bottleneck constraints.
5. The scaling factor α and rank interact non-monotonically: α = 64 with rank8 achieves the minimal gap increase (CG∆ = +0.079), but the same α with
rank-4 degrades to CG∆ = +0.183, indicating that large scaling factors require
sufficient rank capacity.
6. Fine-tuning only the last vision encoder layer jointly with text (Text+VisL1) achieves frozen-level alignment with minimal perturbation: CG remains
at 0.814 (same as frozen) while R@1 reaches 0.700, outperforming both pure
text-only (R@1=0.427) and full both-modality (R@1=0.510). Among the layered fine-tuning configurations, Text+Vis-L1 uses the fewest vision-encoder parameters; deeper visual adaptation (Text+Vis-L4, R@1=0.713) yields marginal
retrieval gains at the cost of perturbing 4× more visual layers.
7. Direct causal evidence supports Visual Fragility: vision encoder noise degrades retrieval 2.6× more than text encoder noise; gradient analysis shows vision gradients are unnecessary for task adaptation; and effective rank tracking
confirms text-only preserves visual embedding geometry.
8. Zero-shot preservation generalizes: text-only retains DTD and Food101 accuracy at or above the frozen baseline, while both-modality consistently degrades
performance.
Our findings align with and provide empirical grounding for recent theoretical advances. The work of [4] proved that dimension collapse is the root cause of the modality gap, and [3] showed that the gap is a structural geometric necessity in multimodal
contrastive learning. Independently, [11] demonstrated from a continual learning perspective that visual encoders exhibit broader parameter distributions and greater variance during fine-tuning than text encoders, making them more vulnerable to perturbation. Our work bridges these theoretical insights with practical fine-tuning strategies,
showing that the simplest intervention—leaving the visual encoder mostly untouched—
is remarkably effective for preserving cross-modal alignment.

2. Related Work
2.1. The Modality Gap in Contrastive VLMs
The modality gap was first systematically characterized by [2], who identified the
Cone Effect—where random initialization restricts each encoder’s output to a narrow
cone—as a primary cause, with the contrastive loss preserving rather than closing this
initial separation. [12] argued for renaming it the “contrastive gap,” showing that the
gap is inherent to the two-encoder InfoNCE loss. Recent theoretical work has deepened
our understanding: [4] provided the first proof that dimension collapse is the fundamental origin of the modality gap. [3] developed a measure-theoretic framework demonstrating that a persistent negative symmetric divergence term makes the modality gap
a structural geometric necessity. [6] identified information imbalance between modalities as a root cause. [5] discovered that CLIP embeddings reside on linearly separable
ellipsoid shells.
On the methodological front, [10] decomposed the modality gap into Centroid Gap
and Distribution Gap components (via per-sample cosine distances after centroid alignment), showing DG is a strong predictor of cross-modal task quality (R2 = 0.986). We
adopt this CG/DG terminology but use a variance-based DG formulation (Section 3)
that is more sensitive to distributional shape changes during PEFT. [13] revealed the
anisotropic structure of the gap. [14] demonstrated that narrowing the inter-modal gap
reduces intra-modal misalignment. [15] proposed lightweight post-pre-training alignment. [16] identified spectral imbalance as a bottleneck in contrastive representation
learning.
2.2. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning for VLMs
LoRA [7] has become the dominant PEFT method for VLMs. [8] discovered rank
imbalance favoring text and FFN, proposing hierarchical budget allocation (HALoRA). [9]
5

introduced implicit modality decomposition (IMoD). [17] proposed Khatri-Rao products for higher effective rank. [18] introduced multi-step flow matching. [19] achieved
full-rank PEFT via random bases. [20] applied LoRA at test time. [21] achieved finegrained alignment with only 0.12% parameters. [22] addressed catastrophic forgetting
during low-rank adaptation.
The vulnerability of visual encoders during fine-tuning has been independently
documented. While [11] studied continual/incremental learning settings, finding that
visual modalities experience broader parameter distributions during class-incremental
training, our work provides complementary evidence from the distinct perspective
of single-task PEFT and modality gap dynamics. [23] proposed collaborative multimodal gradients. [24] advocated frozen encoders with task-specific projections. [25]
addressed domain gaps in small multimodal models via dual-level alignment. [26]
found LoRA outperforms prompt methods by rectifying collapsed attention.
2.3. Gap-Aware and Forgetting-Aware Fine-Tuning
Several works have explored using gap information. [27] proposed Gap-Init. The
IIMM metric [28] predicts fine-tuning outcomes from a single forward pass. [29] directly quantified and reduced the modality gap. [30] studied dynamic representation
optimization for OOD VLMs. [31] showed that fine-tuning erodes safety alignment,
with effects monotonic in LoRA rank.
Evidence is accumulating that vision encoders are the more vulnerable component in VLMs. [32] found MLLMs vulnerable to visual adversarial perturbations. [33]
demonstrated that fine-tuning causes localized parameter shifts in the vision encoder. [34]
identified that a small subset of parameters disproportionately contributes to model
brittleness. [35] quantified “text shortcut learning”—CLIP’s systematic over-reliance
on text over visual evidence—and showed that LoRA-based optimization can reduce
cross-modal dependency drop by 64%. [36] confirmed that VLMs rely predominantly
on textual reasoning, with vision-only accuracy dropping from 92.8% to 12.4% on
matched problems, revealing a persistent modality gap in reasoning. [37] proposed
sparse autoencoder-based regularization that operates only on visual representations,
preventing catastrophic forgetting without text guidance and corroborating that the vi-

6

sual encoder is the component requiring protection. Together with the visual fragility
interpretation, these findings paint a consistent picture: the visual modality is the bottleneck for multimodal robustness.
2.4. Surveys and Broader Context
[38] provided a unified theoretical treatment of uniformity and alignment objectives for multimodal representation learning. Comprehensive surveys [39, 40, 41] document the rapid evolution of VLM architectures, fine-tuning strategies, and the convergence toward MLP-based projection as the dominant connector paradigm.
Within the Pattern Recognition community, cross-modal alignment has seen growing interest. [42] learned unified cross-modal patterns for video grounding. [43] improved ViT generalization through VLM-guided embedding alignment. [44] addressed
missing modality robustness in multi-modal entity alignment via graph transformers.
These works, together with the modality gap analysis presented here, reflect the increasing importance of understanding and controlling cross-modal representation geometry in pattern recognition systems.
3. Methodology
3.1. Modality Gap Metrics
We decompose the modality gap into Centroid Gap (CG) and Distribution Gap
(DG), adopting the terminology of [10, 45] but with a distinct variance-based formulation: whereas Liu et al. define DG via per-sample cosine distances after centroid alignp
ment for training control, our G D = ∥µI − µT ∥2 + ∥σI − σT ∥2 captures distributional
shape statistics suited for PEFT monitoring. Let I = {I1 , . . . , IN } and T = {T 1 , . . . , T N }
be sets of L2-normalized image and text embeddings.
Centroid Gap: GC = ∥µI − µT ∥2 , where µI , µT are modality centroids.
p
Distribution Gap: G D = ∥µI − µT ∥2 + ∥σI − σT ∥2 , assuming diagonal covariance.
We track both metrics at regular intervals (every 2 epochs), recording the initial gap
G(0) before fine-tuning and the gap change ∆G = G(T ) − G(0) .

3.4. DG-based Alignment Monitoring
We propose using the Distribution Gap as an alignment health signal during finetuning. This is motivated by our finding that DG predicts retrieval performance at
r = −0.827 (Section 4.6), while the commonly-used Centroid Gap change (CG∆) has
no significant predictive power (r = 0.206, p = 0.156).
Why DG rather than CG? CG captures only centroid-level shifts between modalities; DG additionally captures changes in the shape of the embedding distributions. Our
partial correlation analysis (DG → R@1, controlling for CG: r = −0.807) confirms that
DG provides information beyond what CG captures. This makes DG uniquely suited
as a monitoring signal.
DG-Guided Early Stopping (DG-ES) as one application. As a concrete instantiation of DG-based monitoring, we implement DG-Guided Early Stopping: at each gap
check interval, if DG rises for P = 2 consecutive checks, training is halted. This rule
requires no labeled validation data and adds zero overhead. DG-ES is one application
of the broader principle; other instantiations (e.g., DG-based learning rate scheduling)
are natural extensions.

5. The Visual Fragility Interpretation
The empirical regularities documented in Section 4 raise a natural question: why
does text-only adaptation overwhelmingly yield smaller alignment degradation than
joint adaptation? We propose the Visual Fragility Interpretation as a parsimonious
account—the pre-trained visual embedding space appears more sensitive to parameter
perturbation than the text embedding space—while emphasizing that this serves the
empirical findings as an organizing framework, not as a standalone theoretical contribution.
5.1. Three Sources of Asymmetry
Dimensional collapse asymmetry. [4] proved that dimension collapse causes the
modality gap. Visual encoders produce embeddings in higher-dimensional subspaces
with richer semantic structure than text encoders; when fine-tuning perturbs these parameters, the higher-dimensional structure collapses more severely.
Information asymmetry. [6] demonstrated that texts contain less information than
paired images, making information imbalance a root cause of the gap. We extend this
to the fine-tuning setting: the text encoder, operating closer to its capacity limit, has
fewer degrees of freedom for perturbation-induced disruption.

distributions and greater variance during fine-tuning than text encoders [11], with lo-

5.3. Limitations and Alternative Explanations
We emphasize that this is an interpretation—a coherent explanatory framework
consistent with converging evidence—rather than a deductively proven theorem. Alternative explanations cannot be fully excluded: the advantage of text-only could stem
from beneficial regularization effects of LoRA’s low-rank constraint on the text encoder, or from gradient concentration in the text branch during contrastive training.
Our Adapter experiments partially rule out LoRA-specific explanations (text-only also
outperforms both on Adapters, which lack low-rank constraints), but data limitations
prevent complete elimination of alternative accounts. We present the visual fragility
interpretation as the most parsimonious account consistent with 116 experimental observations and independent evidence from multiple sources [11, 33, 34, 4, 6], and invite
future work to test its limits.

18

6. Discussion
6.1. Why Does Text-Only Work? A “Visual Fragility” Perspective
Our finding that text-only fine-tuning overwhelmingly yields smaller alignment
degradation than joint fine-tuning—across LoRA, Adapter, BLIP-1, and ViT-L, with
COCO as the sole marginal exception (Table 11)—can be understood through a unified interpretation: the pre-trained visual embedding space appears more susceptible
to perturbation-induced disruption. Three independent lines of evidence are consistent
with this interpretation:
Theoretical inevitability of the gap. [3] proved that the modality gap is a structural necessity in multimodal contrastive learning—a negative symmetric divergence
term in the loss landscape creates a barrier that separates modalities. [4] showed that
dimension collapse is the root cause, and that under subspace constraints, perfect alignment is impossible. These results suggest that the gap is not a defect to be fixed, but a
fundamental property of the embedding geometry that should be preserved.
Empirical fragility of the visual encoder. Independently, [11] demonstrated that
visual encoders exhibit broader parameter distributions and greater variance during
class-incremental learning compared to text encoders, making them more susceptible
to perturbation-induced forgetting. Our results provide complementary evidence from
the modality gap perspective: fine-tuning the visual encoder consistently increases the
gap, while fine-tuning only the text encoder preserves or even reduces it.
Information asymmetry between modalities. [6] showed that texts typically contain less information than paired images, making the contrastive loss force modalities
apart as a way to modulate entropy. This implies that the text encoder operates closer
to its capacity limit, while the visual encoder has more “slack.” Perturbing the visual
encoder therefore risks disrupting a more delicately balanced representation.
Together, these findings suggest the visual fragility interpretation: the pre-trained
visual embedding space is more sensitive to parameter perturbation than the text embedding space, due to a combination of structural gap necessity, information asymmetry, and parameter distribution properties. Text-only fine-tuning is the natural practical
consequence: it achieves task adaptation through the more robust text modality while
preserving the fragile visual representations that underpin cross-modal alignment.
19

6.2. The Gap-Performance Trade-off
An important nuance—and a deliberate emphasis of this study—is the trade-off
between gap preservation and retrieval performance. Text-only fine-tuning yields the
smallest alignment degradation (smallest CG∆), but both-position fine-tuning sometimes achieves higher R@1 (e.g., 0.700 vs. 0.627 for rank-8 on Flickr30k). The
Route D strategy combination experiments make this tension explicit: the configuration that minimizes gap increase (α=64, r=8, text-only, CG∆=+0.079) achieves only
R@1=0.389, well below the best retrieval configuration (R@1=0.702). Summarily,
extreme gap preservation may come at the cost of task adaptation. This suggests that some degree of gap increase may be a side effect of improved task-specific
representations—the modalities drift apart as each specializes to the fine-tuning domain. The strong negative correlation between DG and R@1 (r = −0.827) further indicates that the benefits of joint fine-tuning come at the cost of cross-modal alignment,
which may harm zero-shot generalization and robustness. We therefore recommend
text-only as the default when cross-modal alignment preservation is prioritized—for
instance, when zero-shot capability or embedding-space interpretability matters—and
both-position when raw retrieval accuracy is the sole objective. This aligns with our
central message: protect the visual encoder, fine-tune the text modality.
6.3. Practical Guidelines
Based on our 116 experiments, we offer the following configuration guidelines.
We stress that no single configuration is universally optimal—the choice depends on
whether gap preservation or retrieval accuracy is prioritized.
• For maximum gap preservation: Use text-only LoRA with rank-8, α = 64,
and qv target modules. This limits gap increase to CG∆ = +0.079, though at
reduced retrieval performance (R@1 = 0.389). This configuration is appropriate
when zero-shot capability or embedding interpretability matters more than raw
retrieval accuracy.
• For maximum retrieval performance: Use both-position LoRA with rank-16,
α = 16, and qvko modules (R@1 = 0.702). This configuration trades gap preservation for task adaptation.
20

• For best overall trade-off: Use Text+Vis-L1: text-only LoRA (rank-8, α = 16)
plus LoRA on the last vision encoder layer only. This preserves CG at the frozen
baseline level (0.814) while achieving competitive retrieval (R@1=0.700), using only a single vision layer. Deeper visual adaptation (e.g., Text+Vis-L4,
R@1=0.713) yields marginal retrieval gains at the cost of perturbing more visual layers, while Gap-Init (R@1=0.694, CG∆ = −0.031) offers an alternative
for the both-position setting.
• For Adapter users: Use text-only with large bottleneck dimension (d = 64,
CG∆ = −0.217).
• Caution with large α and low rank: α = 64 with rank-4 degrades alignment
(CG∆ = +0.183); ensure rank ≥ 8 when using large scaling factors (α ≥ 32).
6.4. Limitations
Our study has several limitations. First, we primarily tested on Flickr30k and
COCO; domain-specific datasets may exhibit different gap evolution patterns. Second, our BLIP-1 experiments used selective unfreezing rather than true LoRA injection, due to architectural incompatibility. Third, the SigLIP experiments use a limited
configuration set (5 experiments, plus frozen baseline) and warrant further expansion.
The rank sweep on both-position LoRA yields CG∆ of +0.074 (r=4), +0.078 (r=8),
and +0.072 (r=16). Text-only achieves CG∆ = +0.067, and both-position achieves
CG∆ = +0.058, both within a narrow range (+0.06−0.08). The effect sizes are substantially smaller than on CLIP (where full FT causes CG∆ = +0.199), consistent with
the differing training objectives: the sigmoid loss of SigLIP induces less pronounced
modality separation than CLIP’s contrastive InfoNCE loss, leaving less room for gap
perturbation during fine-tuning. The magnitude of the text-only advantage therefore
depends on the pre-training objective; for SigLIP, both- and text-only configurations
perform comparably. Fourth, while we provide converging empirical evidence for the
visual fragility interpretation, formal theoretical proof connecting gap evolution dynamics to encoder architecture remains open. Fifth, all experiments used a single GPU
(RTX 4060, 8GB), limiting batch sizes and model scales. Sixth, the DMNSP evidence

21

for parameter distribution asymmetry comes from continual learning, and while SEA
provides single-task evidence, a direct measurement of parameter variance changes
during single-task fine-tuning would strengthen the interpretation.
On the strong DG–R@1 correlation. The strong correlation between DG and
R@1 (r = −0.827) warrants explicit discussion. This correlation strength, while substantial, is observed across 81 experiments spanning different methods, architectures,
and datasets. We note that DG mathematically contains CG as a component (DG2
= CG2 + variance term), so the two are not independent metrics. Nevertheless, the
partial correlation of r = −0.807 after controlling for CG confirms that DG captures
meaningful distributional information beyond centroid shift. DG’s added value comes
from the embedding variance term—it captures distributional geometry that CG alone
misses. We note that our correlation analysis treats each experiment as an independent
observation; experiments with shared configurations (e.g., different seeds) are not fully
independent. A mixed-effects model or clustered standard errors would provide a more
conservative estimate, and we encourage future work to adopt such approaches. That
said, we do not claim that this correlation magnitude will generalize to arbitrary VLM
architectures or datasets beyond those tested; it is an empirical regularity observed
within our experimental scope.
On DG-Guided Early Stopping. We tested DG-ES as a proof-of-concept application of DG-based monitoring. While DG-ES triggered correctly on both-position and
low-α configurations, it caused premature stopping on a well-configured text-only run
(R@1=0.133 vs. expected ≈0.63) under the default patience P = 2. We therefore do
not recommend the default instantiation for production use. The broader principle—
that DG can serve as a zero-overhead training-time signal—remains promising, and
more sophisticated instantiations (e.g., DG-based learning rate scheduling) are natural
extensions beyond this study.
We do not claim that the visual fragility interpretation is the only possible explanation for the observed regularities; future work on a broader range of VLMs (e.g., CoCa,
EVA-CLIP), larger-scale datasets, and formal theoretical analysis of gap dynamics under parameter perturbation would further test its limits.

22

6.5. Theory–Method Mutual Validation
The visual fragility interpretation predicts that DG should remain stable under textonly fine-tuning (since the visual encoder is untouched), and that DG-based monitoring
should therefore be unnecessary on well-configured runs. Our Route E experiments
partially support this: DG-ES correctly triggers on configurations that perturb the visual encoder (both, low-α), consistent with the prediction. However, DG-ES also triggers prematurely on the well-configured text-only run—DG itself remains stable, but
the default patience of P = 2 is too sensitive to minor fluctuations. This does not refute
visual fragility (DG is stable under text-only), but it does expose DG-ES as an unreliable instantiation of the monitoring principle in its current form. We therefore do not
claim bidirectional validation between theory and method; rather, the theory provides
a coherent interpretive framework, and DG-ES serves as an illustrative—but not yet
practically robust—application. We do not claim visual fragility as the unique mechanism; rather, it is the most parsimonious account consistent with converging evidence
from retrieval performance, zero-shot preservation, gap dynamics, and causal analysis. Three additional checks support the non-triviality of our metrics: (1) DG remains
significantly correlated with R@1 after controlling for CG (partial r = −0.807), confirming that DG captures distributional geometry beyond centroid shift; (2) CG∆ has
no significant predictive power (r = 0.206, p = 0.156); and (3) the monotonic degradation pattern (frozen → text_only → both → full_ft) holds across two independent
tasks—retrieval and zero-shot classification.
7. Conclusion
We conducted the first systematic study of modality gap evolution during parameterefficient fine-tuning of vision-language models, spanning 116 experiments across two
PEFT methods (LoRA, Adapter), three architectures (CLIP ViT-B/32, ViT-L/14, BLIP1), two retrieval datasets (Flickr30k, MS-COCO), and three zero-shot benchmarks
(CIFAR-100, DTD, Food101). Our work yields five principal contributions.
1. Systematic characterization of gap dynamics under PEFT. Across the full
configuration space, we find that text-only fine-tuning consistently preserves crossmodal alignment, while joint-modality fine-tuning and full fine-tuning systematically
23

degrade it. The effect is robust across LoRA, Adapter, BLIP-1, and ViT-L, and is
statistically significant across five random seeds (p < 0.01). Text-only LoRA limits
Centroid Gap increase to +0.079, compared to +0.199 for full fine-tuning.
2. Distribution Gap as a retrieval predictor. The Distribution Gap exhibits strong
predictive power for retrieval performance (r = −0.827), substantially outperforming
the Centroid Gap (r = −0.683). Crucially, the partial correlation of DG with R@1 remains r = −0.807 after controlling for CG, confirming that DG captures distributional
shape information beyond first-order centroid shift. This is an empirical regularity; we
do not claim it reveals a new mechanism.
3. The Visual Fragility Interpretation. We propose that visual embedding spaces
are inherently more sensitive to parameter perturbation than text spaces—a parsimonious account consistent with independent evidence on dimension collapse asymmetry, information imbalance, and the concentration of trainable parameters in vision encoders. Our causal evidence—parameter noise sensitivity, gradient contribution quantification, and effective rank tracking—provides converging support for this interpretation. We emphasize that Visual Fragility is a phenomenological interpretation, not
a deductively proven mechanism; alternative explanations, including modality-specific
inductive biases in pre-training objectives, cannot be fully excluded.
4. DG-based Alignment Monitoring. The Distribution Gap can serve as a zerooverhead training-time signal for alignment health. As a concrete instantiation, DGGuided Early Stopping halts training when DG rises for two consecutive checks, requiring no labeled validation data. While effective in several configurations, DG-ES
proved brittle under default parameters in well-configured text-only runs, where premature stopping occurred.
5. A gap-aware fine-tuning principle. Across architectures, datasets, and PEFT
methods, the evidence converges on a simple guideline: protect the visual encoder’s
parameters, fine-tune the text modality, and monitor the Distribution Gap as a real-time
health signal. This principle requires no architectural modification and applies to any
contrastive vision-language backbone.
Limitations and future work. First, our study is limited to contrastive dualencoder architectures; whether findings generalize to decoder-based VLMs or non24

contrastive objectives remains open. Second, Visual Fragility lacks a formal mechanistic proof; future theory should derive its emergence conditions. Third, extending
to instruction-tuning, larger datasets, and RLHF would test generality. Fourth, DGGuided Early Stopping requires patience calibration; adaptive thresholding may provide more robust instantiations. Fifth, other PEFT methods (prefix tuning, prompt
tuning, IA3) remain unexplored under our framework.
Outlook. The central practical insight—text-only fine-tuning with moderate rank
as a simple, alignment-preserving baseline—can be adopted immediately by practitioners. Distribution-level alignment monitoring may generalize beyond retrieval to VQA
and captioning, where alignment degradation manifests in measurable forms. We hope
this empirical foundation contributes to more principled parameter-efficient adaptation
of vision-language models.

D. DG-ES Implementation Details
DG-Guided Early Stopping is a training callback. DG is computed every 2 epochs from 5,000 validation
image-text pairs. Patience P = 2 triggers a stop after two consecutive gap checks (4 epochs) with monotonically increasing DG. For well-configured text-only runs, we recommend disabling DG-ES (P → ∞) to
prevent premature stopping.

E. Gap Metrics Non-Triviality Checks
Three lines of evidence confirm DG is not a redundant proxy for CG:
1. Partial correlation: DG remains significant after controlling for CG (partial r = −0.807, n = 81,
p < 0.001).
2. CG∆ non-predictive: CG∆ shows no significant correlation with R@1 (r = 0.206, p = 0.156).
3. Construct validity: DG2 = CG2 + ∥σI − σT ∥2 ; the variance component independently correlates
with R@1.

F. Weight-Level and Spectral Evidence
To complement the geometric interpretation in Section 5, we provide weight-level and spectral analysis
comparing the frozen (baseline), text-only (rank-8, α=16), and full fine-tuning configurations on Flickr30k.
Figure 8 presents a four-panel summary.

Figure 8: Weight and spectral evidence. (A) Mean ∥∆W∥F per encoder: text-only has zero vision-side
updates, non-zero text-side updates (∥∆W∥txt = 0.66). Full FT perturbs all parameters. (B) Image embedding
singular value spectra: text-only overlaps with Frozen (σ1 = 16.15); Full FT inflates the spectrum (σ1 =
19.12). (C) Text embedding spectra: both methods modify the text space (σ1 : 15.88 → 12.45 and 15.88 →
19.62). (D) Frozen image effective rank is preserved under text-only but degrades under Full FT.
