Abstract
Fine-tuning a vision-language model on a small corpus often widens the image–
text gap. We first characteriz when this happens. Acrossn= 204archived
runs drawn from six dataset×backbone cells, the Distribution Gap predicts
retrieval degradation (r=−0.71) better than the Centroid Gap (r=−0.59),
and which part of the model is adapted matters more than how its adapters
are configured. We then deriveGap Susceptibilityfrom gradient flow: with
the alignment energy as the state variable, the rate of gap change is the task
gradient projected onto the alignment gradient, yielding an ODE with a PEFT-
stable restoring coefficient that reproduces the measured gap trajectory at mean
R2 = 0.949over LoRA, against0.00for a constant baseline. Validation spans
383 experiments over six backbones and three PEFT methods. A strict-freezing
control that trains only adapter matrices confirms the predicted ordering on
CLIP and reverses it on SigLIP, so the text-only advantage is architectural, not
universal. Tuning each method to its own learning rate removes the adapter
methods’ apparent advantage: LoRA and full fine-tuning then agree on zero-
shot accuracy (60.69%against60.87%). Because the runs are nested rather
than independent, we report the gap–retrieval association with cluster-robust
inference: the slope is−1.05(CR1 standard error0.29,p= 0.016), the family-

1. Introduction
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) of vision-language models has be-
come a standard practice in multimodal machine learning. Methods like
LoRA Hu et al. (2022) and Adapter Houlsby et al. (2019) enable adaptation
of billion-parameter models to downstream tasks with minimal computational
cost. Yet a fundamental question remains largely unanswered:how does fine-
tuning affect the cross-modal alignment that these models were pre-trained to
achieve?
Recent empirical work has suggested a striking regularity: that text-only
fine-tuning preserves cross-modal alignment better than joint-modality fine-
tuning. This paper examines that suggestion across 383 experiments on five
architectures (Section 3) and finds it holds where the comparison isolates the
adaptation site, but not universally: under strict freezing the ordering reverses
on one of the three backbones tested, so which site is preferable has to be
measured rather than assumed. The Distribution Gap (DG)—a measure of dis-
tributional mismatch between visual and text embeddings—predicts retrieval
performance atr=−0.714overn= 204experiments, far outperforming the
commonly reported Centroid Gap. These findings, collectively termed the Vi-
sual Fragility Interpretation, suggest that intervening on the visual side is what
damages alignment. Whether the vision tower is intrinsically more sensitive is
a separate question that we test and answer negatively under matched relative
perturbation (Section 3), so we state the claim about placement rather than
about fragility. But they leave open a deeper question:why does this hap-
pen?What is the governing mechanism behind modality gap dynamics under
fine-tuning?

Together, these contributions establish a principled framework for under-
standing and predicting how parameter-efficient fine-tuning reshapes cross-
modal representation geometry. The practical message is correspondingly qual-
ified: where the encoders are InfoNCE-trained and the modality gap is the

quantity of interest, adapting the text side while leaving the visual side alone

retrieval can point in different directions.
2. Related Work
2.1. The Modality Gap in Contrastive VLMs
Random initialization leaves each encoder inside a narrow cone, and the
contrastive objective tends to preserve that arrangement rather than collapse it;
the observation is known as the Cone Effect Liang et al. (2022) and remains the
earliest systematic treatment ofthe gap. Fahim et al.(2024) argued forrenaming
it the “contrastive gap,” showing that the gap is inherent to the two-encoder
InfoNCE loss. Later theory sharpened the picture. Yi et al. (2025) proved that
dimension collapse is its fundamental origin. Cai et al. (2026) showed within a
measure-theoretic framework that a persistent negative symmetric divergence
term turns the gap into astructural geometric necessity. Schrodi et al. (2025)
traced a root cause to information imbalance between the modalities, and Levi
and Gilboa (2025) found that CLIP’s embeddings occupy linearly separable
ellipsoid shells.
Splitting the gap into a centroid and a distribution part is not new. Per-
sample cosine distances, taken after the centroids have been aligned, yield a
distributional term that predicts cross-modal task quality well (R2 = 0.986)
Liu et al. (2026). The quantity we call DG differs: ours is built on variances
(Section 3), which makes it more responsive to distributional shape changes
introduced by PEFT. The anisotropic structure of the gap was mapped by Yu
et al. (2026); Mistretta et al. (2025) showed that narrowing the inter-modal
gap also reduces misalignment within modalities; and Guo et al. (2026) named
spectral imbalance as a bottleneck for contrastive representation learning.

2.2. Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning for VLMs
LoRA Hu et al. (2022) has become the dominant PEFT method for
VLMs. Zhang et al. (2026a) discovered rank imbalance favoring text and FFN,
proposing hierarchical budget allocation (HALoRA). Zhang et al. (2026b) in-
troduced implicit modality decomposition (IMoD), motivated by the obser-
vation that adaptable parameters in multimodal models are dominated by
the text modality, leaving non-text modalities under-trained. Contemporary
work includes Khatri-Rao adapters Albert et al. (2025a), flow matching Jiang
et al. (2026), random-basis full-rank PEFT Albert et al. (2025b), and test-time
LoRA Kojima et al. (2025). Guo et al. (2025) achieved fine-grained alignment
with only 0.12% parameters.
Independent of anything reported here, the visual encoder has repeatedly
emerged as the more exposed component during adaptation: under class-
incremental training, for instance, visual modalities are the ones observed to
develop broader parameter distributions as classes are added. Zhou et al. (2025)
advocated frozen encoders with task-specific projections. Dayal et al. (2026)
addressed domain gaps in small multimodal models via dual-level alignment.
Our mechanism probes (Section 3) report the corresponding single-task PEFT
evidence, together with a strict control that localises the alignment benefit to
where the adapters are placed rather than to vision-side updates.
That last observation deserves a direct comparison with the present results,
because the two point in apparently opposite directions and the difference is
instructive. Zhang et al. (2026b) treat text dominance as a pathology to be
corrected, whereas the design patterns of Section 3 recommend leaning on the
text side. Three differences account for most of the distance between them.
Theirsetting addsmodalityadapters toalargelanguagemodel, whereanunder-
trained non-text modality has a shared decoder to degrade; the encoder pairs
studied here are dual-tower and contrastive, and the text-side arm simply leaves
the visual tower untouched. Their correction is a decomposition of where the
parameters live, which our experiments do not vary. And their evidence is task
performance, where the cost of an under-trained modality shows up directly,
6
while ours is the geometry of the two embedding spaces, which can move without
retrieval following. What the two agree on is the diagnosis: adaptation that
concentrates on the text side changes the two encoders unevenly. Where this
paper adds to theirs is in showing that the consequence of that imbalance is
not fixed — the same text-side intervention reduces the gap on one pre-trained
pair and increases it on two others — so neither the enthusiasm of the design
patterns nor the concern of Zhang et al. (2026b) transfers across backbones
without measurement.
Three recent accounts bear directly on how the gap should be treated, and
takentogethertheynarrowthequestionratherthanwidenit. Yarasetal.(2026)
analyz how the gaparisesduring contrastive pre-training through gradient-flow
dynamics, locating the cause in mismatched pairs and a learnable temperature,
and report that closing the gap improves retrieval; their intervention is applied
while the encoders are being trained together, which is a different stage from
the one studied here, where the encoders are already fixed and only an adapter
changes. Sharma and Saxena (2026) work at the downstream stage and on
both CLIP and SigLIP, and find that interventions on the gap help some tasks
and hurt others: subtracting a fixed offset is equivalent to a bias term for zero-
shot classification, whereas projecting the gap direction out distorts retrieval
rankings. Chen et al. (2026) arrive at a related asymmetry from the language-
model side, arguing that cross-modal alignment is an asymmetric projection
onto a rigid text manifold and that visual-side adjustment is what damages
it. The present results are consistent with all three and sharpen the practical
conclusion: thegapisnotanobjectivethatcanbeminimizonbehalfofretrieval,
because which adaptation site produces the smaller gap and which produces the
better retrieval need not be the same site at all.
2.3. Gap-Aware and Gradient-Informed Fine-Tuning
Gap information has been used to steer fine-tuning before. Zhao et al. (2026)
introduced Gap-Init, an initialization that aligns LoRA with the gap vector. It
and our discrepancy measureΓm rest on the same observation—that the gap
7
dominates gradient flow—but diverge in purpose: theirs is an engineering de-
vice, ours a dynamical account that predicts how the gap evolves from initial-
ization. The IIMM metric Niss et al. (2025) predicts fine-tuning outcomes from
a single forward pass through inter- and intra-modal similarity measures. Role
et al. (2025) directly quantified and reduced the modality gap. Sun et al. (2026)
showedthatfine-tuningerodessafetyalignment, witheffectsmonotonicinLoRA
rank.
The visual half of the model is the more fragile one, and this now has sup-
port from three directions that were established independently of each other:
multimodal LLMs prove vulnerable to adversarial perturbations applied on the
visual side Malik et al. (2025); vision-side parameters move in localised fashion
when the model is fine-tuned Wang et al. (2025); and the brittleness itself con-
centrates in a parameter subset small enough to name Dai et al. (2026). Zhou
(2026) quantified “text shortcut learning”—CLIP’s systematic over-reliance on
text over visual evidence—and showed that LoRA-based optimization can re-
duce cross-modal dependency drop by 64%. Xu et al. (2026) confirmed that
VLMs rely predominantly on textual reasoning, with vision-only accuracy drop-
ping from 92.8% to 12.4% on matched problems. Morelli et al. (2026) proposed
sparse autoencoder-based regularization operating only on visual representa-
tions, corroborating that the visual encoder requires protection. Read together
these results motivate the visual term of our ODE, but—as Section 3 shows
with a strict control—they do not establish that perturbing the visual encoder
is what produces the alignment benefit.
2.4. What This Paper Adds
Three strands of prior work bound what is new here. The first explains
the origin of the modality gap: Liang et al. (2022) trace it to the contrastive
objective and the embedding-space geometry it induces, Fahim et al. (2024)
argue that the relevant quantity is a contrastive rather than a genuine modality
separation, and Schrodi et al. (2025) separate the gap from object bias. Those
accounts are static: they say what the gap is, not how it moves while a model
8
is adapted. The second strand uses gap-derived quantities to drive training —
Gap-Init and related initialisation schemes at the start, and Liu et al. (2026)
for training control, both of which presuppose that the gap is the right state
variable. The third reports asymmetries between the two towers without a
dynamical account of them (Niss et al. (2025) predicts fine-tuning outcomes
from a single forward pass). What this paper adds is the step none of them
takes: we treat the gap as the state of a dynamical system, derive the evolution
equation from gradient flow, verify its coefficients against measured trajectories,
and then report the two places where the derivation’s own predictions fail — the
absolute value of the restoring coefficient disagrees in sign with the fitted one,
and the trajectory-averaged driving term does not reproduce the arm ordering.
A falsified prediction is part of the contribution rather than an omission from
it, because it delimits the regime in which the state-variable view can be used.
2.5. Surveys and Broader Context
Yin et al. (2026) gave a unified theoretical treatment of uniformity and
alignment for multimodal representation learning, while the surveys Rahman
(2026); Chen et al. (2025); Zhu et al. (2025) chart how VLM architectures and
fine-tuning strategies have evolved. Within the Pattern Recognition community,
recent work has explored cross-modal alignment for video grounding Yang and
Wei (2026), VLM-guided ViT generalization Wang et al. (2026), and missing-
modality entity alignment Yang et al. (2026), reflecting growing interest in cross-
modal representation geometry.

3.5. Visual Fragility: an Interpretation of Where the Adapters Sit, Not of a
Fragile Tower
Why does text-only preserve alignment? We propose theVisual Fragility
Interpretation: intervening on the visual side of a vision-language model is
what damages cross-modal alignment, whereas leaving it untouched preserves it.
Two evidence lines support this interpretation, and one earlier line is withdrawn
in favor of them.
Noise sensitivity (withdrawn).An earlier single evaluation in which
each encoder tensor was perturbed by Gaussian noise scaled to a fixed multiple
of that tensor’s own standard deviation (σstd(Wi); despite a filter intended to
exclude them, LayerNorm parameters were perturbed too, and they carry73.9%
of the vision tower’s injected Frobenius norm) suggested that vision-side noise
degrades retrieval2.6×more than text-side noise. Repeating the probe with the
image set and the augmentation draw held fixed across conditions, and with a
σ=0control that reproduces the baseline exactly, gives no stable ordering over
five seeds (1.90±1.83%for the vision encoder against2.48±3.25%for the text
encoder atσ=0.1, pairedp= 0.77; the ratio of means is0.77, i.e. the text side is
nominally the more sensitive of the two). This evidence line is therefore **not
used**, and theσ=0control is retained in the released artefact.
Gradient contribution.We measure the gradient signal-to-noise ratio
(GSNR) for vision and text encoders during fine-tuning. During joint-modality
fine-tuning the vision encoder’s gradient norm exceeds the text encoder’s by
1.41×(0.528against0.383, single measurement on the reference configuration),
indicating that visual parameters experience stronger optimization pressure un-
der joint training. We report the measured ratio rather than a rounded one,
and note that it rests on a single measurement.
Effective rank under strict control.We track the effective rank of
the per-layer image embeddings under a strictly frozen protocol, in which the
optimizer sees only the adapter matrices (196,608trainable parameters for the
text-only arm) and the vision side registers zero non-zero updates across all

3.7. Placement Against Prior Work
Individual studies have argued that the choice of adaptation site outweighs
how a fixed budget is divided within it in the low-data regime, and report gains
from siting adaptation so that the vision tower is left unchanged rather than
from re-allocating rank among its modules Zhang et al. (2023); Wang et al.
(2024); Zanella and Ben Ayed (2024). What those reports establish is that the
choice matters; they do not establish how far the resulting ordering generaliz,
because each is measured on one backbone and one task corpus. We therefore
treat the ordering as a hypothesis rather than a premise and measure the same
three arms on seven backbones under a protocol in which the optimiz sees only
21
the adapter matrices. The measurements above show that the ordering follows
the pre-training objective rather than vision-language adaptation in general:
every contrastively pre-trained backbone, at either patch size, puts text-only at
the smallest gap change, while the two backbones trained with other objectives
put both-positions there. Section 4 derives the mechanism that produces it.
3.8. Design Patterns
Synthesizing the empirical findings, three design patterns emerge, and none
of them is the unconditional recipe it is often taken to be. (1)Adaptation
position: the ordering is stable within an architecture, but which end
of it is preferable is not.Under strict freezing the same three arms are
ordered identically on the three contrastive backbones, whether they use patch
size32or16, as Table 2 records. An earlier reading of these runs attributed the
ordering to patch size, because the two backbones that had been measured at
patch size16both placed the both-position arm smallest. That reading does not
survive the matched comparison: CLIP B/16 and CLIP B/32, measured at the
same rank under the same protocol, order the arms identically despite differing
in patch size by a factor of two. What separates the two orderings is the pre-
training objective. Every backbone trained contrastively on image–text pairs
places the text-only arm at the smallest gap change, across three pre-training
corpora and both patch sizes; the two backbones trained otherwise — SigLIP,
whose objective is a pairwise sigmoid rather than a softmax over the batch,
and BioCLIP, whose corpus is biological — place the both-position arm there
instead. The added backbone is BioCLIP, a biological-domain model, and two
caveats follow from that. It was measured at rank4only, and it begins from a
gapof1.79onnaturalimageswheretheB/32backbonesbeginat0.81, sodomain
shift alone could account for its ordering; and with two backbones per patch size
the two are not separated. We therefore report the grouping as a regularity over
seven configurations rather than as a property of patch size. On the two B/32
backbones the arm that minimiz the gap (text-only) is the one that retrieves
worst, so the ordering by gap runs opposite to the ordering by retrieval; on
22
SigLIP the two orderings agree and both place the both-position arm first. The
practical reading is that no adaptation site transfers across backbones without
measurement. (2)Therankeffectisprotocol-dependent.Undertheearlier
protocol, in which the rest of the backbone remains trainable, ranks4–16reduce
the gap and ranks32and64increase it, which is where the familiar “lower
rank is safer” rule comes from. Under strict freezing, where the optimiz sees
only the adapter matrices, the text-only arm improves monotonically with rank
instead, reaching−0.075atr=32against−0.009atr=1and−0.029atr=4,
and the both-position arm is non-monotonic, turning positive byr=64. The
rule is therefore an artefact of what else was being trained, not a property of
rank. The direction is not specific to one backbone. Pairing rank8against
rank4within seeds, the gap falls on CLIP at the text-only and both-positions
(t=−8.0and−2.9) and on SigLIP likewise (t=−3.8and−2.6); the exception
is OpenCLIP at the both-position, which moves the other way by0.004and
att= +1.1is not distinguishable from no change. Five of six combinations
therefore agree, and the dissenting one is the one that carries no significance.
(3)Full fine-tuning is not unconditionally destructive, but it is far
more learning-rate sensitive than the adapter methods—at the shared

4.5. Effective Rank Saturation
The ODE framework yields an initially counterintuitive prediction. If LoRA
rankrestrictstheadaptationsubspace,∥g v∥shouldgrowwithrankas∥g v∥∝ √r
under random projection onto a rank-rsubspace. We tested this prediction and
found itfalsified:∥g v∥varies by only10%acrossr∈[1,64](12.75–14.09).
This falsification reveals a deeper insight: the task gradient’s effective di-
mensionr eff is determined by task structure, not configured rank. The task gra-
dient concentrates in a low-dimensional subspace across the adapter capacities
we probed (rankrfrom1to64). We term thisEffective Rank Saturation,
and it implies that beyond a minimal rank threshold, increasing LoRA rank
provides no additional gradient capacity—only the risk of disrupting alignment
through expanded parameter freedom. This finding has implications beyond
our framework, suggesting that commonly used moderate ranks (r≥16) may
be excessive for many vision-language tasks.

6.1. Why Gap Susceptibility Matters
Gap SusceptibilityΓm =∥∇ mDG∗∥·cosϕ m is not a proposed heuristic to be
compared against alternatives—it is the coupling coefficient thatmustgovern
gap dynamics if gradient flow is the underlying mechanism. The question is
not whetherΓm is a “good” predictor but whether the ODE it generates fits the

data. Theanswer, asSection5shows, isaffirmativefortheLoRAconfigurations:
meanR 2 = 0.949(range0.814–0.977) across the archived CLIP runs, fitted one

CLIP full-fine-tuning pilot below both, at0.922; both are reported in Section 5.
This framework stands apart from prior approaches along three dimensions.
First, unlikeIIMMNissetal.(2025), whichpredictsfine-tuningoutcomesfroma

6.2. Practical Implications for PEFT Design
The ODE framework provides quantitative guidance for PEFT configuration
selection. For practitioners, three design principles follow:
1.Usetext-onlyfine-tuningasthedefault.Thetheoryshowsthisisnot
an empirical quirk but a consequence ofgv = 0eliminating the dominant
perturbation source. Text-only requires no additional implementation.
We report the protection it provides as an empirical tendency over our
archive rather than a guarantee: the gap inflation is smaller for text-only
in most of our configurations and the ordering reverses on SigLIP under
strict freezing, so the rule should be verified per backbone.
2.Choose minimal effective rank.Effective Rank Saturation implies
thatr≥4suffices for the tested tasks; larger ranks expand the perturba-
tion subspace without adding gradient capacity. The common practice of
settingr= 16or higher may be excessive.

3.Monitor DG as an alignment health signal.The ODE establishes

final R@1:r=−0.71,n= 96). It follows that DG is worth tracking as

6.4. Connection to Pattern Recognition
The cross-modal alignment dynamics studied here exemplify a broader class
of pattern recognition problems: identifying systematic regularities in how
training design choices map to representation outcomes across-architectures,
datasets, and objectives. The ODE framework demonstrates that these regu-
larities are not arbitrary but follow from the geometry of gradient coupling be-
tween task optimization and representation structure. We hope this principled
approach to understanding representation dynamics finds application beyond
the specific context of vision-language alignment.
7. Conclusion
This paper has established a complete arc from empirical discovery to the-
oretical explanation to experimental verification, spanning 383 experiments
across six backbones (CLIP ViT-B/32, CLIP ViT-B/16, CLIP ViT-L/14, Open-
CLIP laion2B, SigLIP, and BLIP-1), three PEFT methods, and two retrieval
datasets. Our contributions are threefold.
First, we presented a systematic empirical characterization of modality gap
evolution under PEFT, covering 117 mechanism experiments and 104 strict-
freezing controls. That work established that adaptation at the text position
preserves cross-modal alignment on the InfoNCE-based encoders studied (a di-
rection we show reverses on SigLIP), that the Distribution Gap predicts retrieval
performance (r=−0.714overn= 204experiments; clustered-bootstrap esti-
matesr=−0.738, 95% CI:[−0.889,−0.613], and independentlyr=−0.847,
95% CI:[−0.936,−0.709]), and that what damages cross-modal alignment is
intervening on the visual side rather than adapting the language side (Visual
Fragility Interpretation, supported by strict-freezing controls; we show under
matched relative perturbation that this is a statement about where the adapters
sit, not evidence that the vision tower is intrinsically the more fragile one).
43
Second, we derived Gap SusceptibilityΓm =∥∇ mDG∗∥·cosϕ m from gra-
dient flow and obtained the first-order ODEd(DG∗)/dt=α−β(DG ∗−DG 0).
This ODE provides a unified explanation for the empirical regularities: the text-
only position follows fromgv = 0removing the dominant perturbation source
for a given allocation; LoRA’s protective effect fromα<0for all PEFT configu-
rations; and the rank effect from Effective Rank Saturation—the discovery that
task gradient effective dimension is determined by task structure (reff≪64),
not configured rank.
Third, we validated the ODE across 55 archived LoRA runs (49 on Flickr30k,
6 on MS-COCO), each fitted separately. The framework reproduces gap trajec-
tories at meanR 2 = 0.949over the LoRA configurations (range0.814–0.977),
substantially outperforming the constant baseline, andαis negative on both
datasets. Across the215fits we archived,α <0in196; the19exceptions are
fourteenofthefiftyAdapterfits(largest+0.061), fourLoRAfitsfittedleave-one-

i.e. numerically zero), so we state the protective bound as the dominant rather
than a universal regime, andβis stable across PEFT and full fine-tuning under
matched settings (βfull = 0.159).
The practical message is narrower than a blanket recipe, and the ODE is
what narrows it. Where the modality gap is the quantity of interest and the
encoders are InfoNCE-trained, leaving the visual side alone and adapting the
text side is the safer default, and the mechanism says why: withgv = 0the
dominant perturbation term vanishes. That reasoning does not transfer auto-
matically. On SigLIP the same control reverses the ordering, so the recipe has
to be established per architecture rather than assumed. A second caution cuts
across-architectures: a smaller gap and better retrieval are not the same objec-
tive. Both-position adaptation retrieves better on CLIP and on SigLIP while
reducing the gap more on only one of them, so monitoring the gap alone can
point away from the configuration that retrieves best.

C. ODE Framework: Theorem Verification and Falsification
A note on the theoreticalβformula: applying Equation (8)’s definition ofβdirectly to
the measured gradients yieldsβtheor≈0.0035, roughly40×smaller than the fitted values
(β≈0.14–0.16). Theαprediction is therefore the primary quantitative test of the framework
(verified below), while a fully predictive theory of the restoring coefficient requires additional
modeling (e.g., gradient-noise or higher-order effects) and is left to future work.
The Theorem 4.1 prediction—αpred =−η P
m ¯Γm∥gm∥—agrees with the empirically fit-
tedα obs in sign and order of magnitude across all six pilot configurations (all negative, within
0.03 of each other), although rank ordering is not preserved at this sample size. The variance
of this estimator is worth recording, because it is large and it dictates howΓm must be mea-
sured in practice. Estimates of P
m⟨∇mDG∗,gm⟩formed from a single batch of64or128
pairs spread over roughly−86to+72with a standard deviation of27–49, against a mean of
18–32, so a batch-level estimate carries a signal-to-noise ratio near unity and its sign is not
stable. The consequence is thatΓ m should be estimated bypairedmeasurement—forming
the predicted step−η⟨∇θDG∗,∇θL⟩and comparing it to the change an actual optimiz step
produces at the same state, as done above, where the correlation reaches0.998—rather than
by averaging independent batch-level estimates, which requires of order102–103 batches to
reach10%relative precision. We report this because it bounds how the framework can be
used: the ODE’s coefficients are obtained by fitting each trajectory, which is deterministic,
and the gradient-level quantities above are diagnostic of the mechanism rather than a practical
route to predictingαandβin advance.

D. Cross-Dataset ODE Validation
An earlier version of this supplement reported ODE coefficients for MS-COCO in a table.
That table has been withdrawn. Its coefficients were fitted toDG∗ trajectories from runs in
which the backbone remained trainable, and the strict-freezing runs this paper’s controls use
do not reproduce the quantity it measured.
The reason is measurable and is reported here rather than left implicit. Under strict
freezing theDG∗ trajectory on MS-COCO moves by0.003–0.006over ten epochs, whereas
the centroid gap moves by0.108–0.111on the same runs; the ratio of the two spans is0.030–
0.055. On Flickr30k the same ratio is1.2–1.6. Fitting the exponential form to the MS-COCO
trajectory givesR 2 = 0.12and0.68on the two seeds available, against0.93for the centroid
gap on those same runs. The retrieval accuracy explains why: strict freezing on MS-COCO
reachesR@1 = 0.12where Flickr30k reaches0.62–0.70, so the model has moved the two
modality centroids toward one another without bringing matched pairs together. A centroid
gap can narrow while paired embeddings stay apart, andDG∗ measures the latter. Where
DG∗ does not move, the ODE is fitted to noise.
MS-COCO is therefore retained in this paper for what the strict-freezing runs do support
— the comparison of adaptation sites by centroid gap, reported in the main text and in
Section 5 — and not for ODE coefficients.
E. Decoder-Based VLM
The main text’s claim about adaptation site is established on contrastive dual-encoder
models. To test whether it depends on that architecture family, we repeated the strict-
freezing comparison on a decoder-based VLM, Qwen2-VL-2B-Instruct, where the two towers
are separated by the fact that their linear layers carry different names: the vision tower
usesattn.qkv,attn.projandmlp.fc1/fc2, and the language model uses the usual seven
projections. Passing different target modules to the adapter library therefore isolates the
towers, and this was verified before any training: the text-only arm holds392adapters and no
vision-side tensor, the vision-only arm holds258and no language-side tensor, and the both-
position arm holds both sets. Every run freezes the backbone and exposes only the adapter
matrices.
The protocol is400samples for three epochs at batch2, rank8and learning rate10 −4,
and every configuration is run at thirteen seeds; we report means with the spread across
seeds. Precision is an issue we had to settle before the comparison could be made. Our first
runs used four-bit quantisation, which is not reproducible: repeating one configuration with
an identical seed changes the measured gap span from0.304to0.407, because the quantised
matrix multiplications are not deterministic. The same configuration in bfloat16 reproduces
bit for bit (0.110 064on both of two repeated runs). All results below are therefore bfloat16.

which adaptation site is preferable; that vision-only is the worst of the three, in contrast, is
supported by every seed.

two, with its largest value below the smallest value either other arm reaches. Second, and in
contrast to the dual-encoder result, the ordering between the two stronger arms is not resolved
on this model: they differ by0.86pooled standard deviations on the centroid gap and their

seed ranges overlap, so thirteen seeds are not enough to separate them. The evidence remains
preliminary — one model, small scale, and a training pipeline whose quantised variant we
could not reproduce — but it is enough to say that on a decoder VLM the site that is weakest
